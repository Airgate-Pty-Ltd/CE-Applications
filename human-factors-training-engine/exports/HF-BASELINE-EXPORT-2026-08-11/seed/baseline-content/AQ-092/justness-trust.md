# Lesson 0.1 — Welcome, and Why Just Culture Exists

## 7. The teach-out

Let me start with the moment this whole course is about. Something goes wrong — a part gets fitted the wrong way round, a step gets skipped, a check gets missed. It lands on a leader's desk. And in the next ten minutes, that leader makes a decision that will quietly shape their entire safety system: *what do we do about the person involved?* Get that decision right, consistently, and people keep telling you the truth. Get it wrong, even once in a visible way, and you start training your whole workforce to go quiet. That decision — made fairly and consistently, over and over — **is** just culture.

So let me define it before we lean on the term. **Just culture** is the discipline of responding to error and risk-taking *fairly and consistently* — distinguishing between honest human error, at-risk behaviour, and reckless behaviour, and responding to each one appropriately. It is not a mood, and it is not a slogan. It's a repeatable way of deciding how to respond when something goes wrong, so the response fits the *behaviour* rather than the *luck of the outcome*.

Now here's the idea I want you to carry through the entire course, because it's the one most organizations get wrong. There is a **false choice** that traps a lot of well-meaning leaders. On one side sits the old **blame culture** — "find who did it, punish them, move on." On the other side, reacting against that, sits a **blame-free culture** — "we never discipline anyone for a safety issue; everyone's protected, no exceptions." And people argue about which one is right as if those are the only two options. They're not. **Both of those extremes fail**, for opposite reasons, and we'll prove that in the next lesson. Just culture is the **middle path**: not blame-everyone, not blame-nobody, but a disciplined distinction that supports honest people and still holds genuine recklessness to account.

> **STOP & THINK**
>
> When was the last time you saw "what happens to the person" decided by the *outcome* rather than the *behaviour* — where a small mistake that happened to cause a big mess got hammered, while an identical mistake that got lucky got a shrug? Hold that. That mismatch — judging the result instead of the choice — is the single most common way a fairness system breaks, and it's what this whole course teaches you to fix.

A word on how the course runs, so there are no surprises. There are six sections. We start with **why both extremes fail** (the false choice), then the **three behaviours** — human error, at-risk behaviour, reckless behaviour — one section each, taught the same way every time. Then a section on **applying it consistently** — the decision process, the substitution test, system-versus-individual, the hard cases and the manager pitfalls. Each section ends with a graded knowledge check at eighty percent. The course ends with a final exam at eighty percent and a graded practical where you classify a set of AeroMach behaviours and decide the just response to each.

And the reassurance that pairs with that bar: **if you engage with the AeroMach situations and actually practise the decision, you will not have a problem.** The standard is set high because a fairness system you apply *inconsistently* is worse than none at all — inconsistency is itself a kind of unfairness. Clearing the bar is the point.

## 8. Industry example

At AeroMach, Dana — the Safety Manager — inherited a system that swung between the two extremes depending on who was angry that week. A messy escape would get someone written up; a quiet near-miss that happened to hurt no one would get ignored. Reports were drying up, and Dana could see why: people had learned that putting your hand up was a gamble on the boss's mood. Priya, newly promoted to team lead, is about to face her first "what do we do about Sam" moment — and she has no framework for it. That's the stake. This course is the framework Dana wishes she'd had, and the one Priya needs by Friday.

## 15. Knowledge Check — graded • 3 questions • 80% to pass

**Q1. Just culture is best described as:**
- A) A policy of never disciplining anyone for a safety issue
- B) The discipline of responding to error and risk-taking fairly and consistently — distinguishing human error, at-risk behaviour, and reckless behaviour, and responding to each appropriately
- C) A faster way to find who's at fault
- D) An HR procedure for handling complaints

> **Best answer: B** — Just culture is the *disciplined distinction* between behaviours, applied consistently. It is neither blame-everyone nor blame-nobody.

**Q2. The "false choice" at the heart of just culture is:**
- A) Whether to have an SMS at all
- B) The belief that the only two options are a pure blame culture or a pure blame-free culture
- C) Whether to report internally or to the regulator
- D) Whether safety or production comes first

> **Best answer: B** — Most leaders think they must pick blame-everyone or blame-nobody. Just culture is the middle path between those two failed extremes.

**Q3. The decision this course is really about is:**
- A) How to write a better procedure
- B) How a leader responds when something goes wrong — fairly, consistently, fitting the response to the behaviour rather than the outcome
- C) How to calculate a risk score
- D) How to discipline faster

> **Best answer: B** — The whole course is about that "what do we do about the person" decision, made to fit the behaviour and the choice, not the luck of the result.
