# Section 2 — The Capacity & Condition Precursors

> **Section goal**
>
> Teach the second four — the ones that degrade your *capacity* to do the work or reflect the *physical reality* you're working in: complacency, fatigue, stress, and lack of resources. The learner leaves able to define each, spot it, state the consequence, and name the safeguard.

**LAYER B — TEACH THE SUBJECT**

### 7. The teach-out

These two precursors share a nasty property: **you are the worst possible judge of them in yourself.** The complacent person feels confident. The fatigued person feels "fine." That's exactly why they need *external* safeguards, not just self-awareness.

**Complacency.** *Define it:* over-confidence born of routine — "I've done this a thousand times, it's always fine." It's the quiet erosion of vigilance on a familiar task. *Why it bites:* familiarity feels like competence, but it actually *lowers* your inspection effort. You stop genuinely looking and start confirming what you expect to see. On the thousand-and-first repetition — the one with the defect — you give it the same glance as the nine hundred good ones. *How it shows up at AeroMach:* an experienced inspector signs off a part type he's passed a thousand times and doesn't truly *look* this time — and this is the one with the crack. *Consequence:* a real defect waved through by an expert, precisely *because* he's an expert who stopped expecting to find anything. *The safeguard:* **inspect expecting to find a fault — make yourself prove it's good.** Approach every inspection as if there *is* a defect and your job is to find it. Use the checklist even when you "know" the task. Never sign for work you didn't personally verify. Routine is the enemy of vigilance, so you build the vigilance into the *process*, not the mood.

**Fatigue.** *Define it:* degraded mental and physical performance from lack of sleep, long hours, circadian disruption (night shift), or sustained effort. *Why it bites:* fatigue silently attacks exactly the things maintenance needs most — attention, working memory, judgement, and reaction time — and here's the dangerous part: **fatigue degrades the very faculty you'd use to judge how fatigued you are.** A tired person genuinely believes they're performing fine while making more errors. It's not laziness; it's physiology. *How it shows up at AeroMach:* a strong technician on his third consecutive long shift misses a verification step on a flight-critical assembly — a step he'd never miss rested. *Consequence:* an error from a good person whose brain was running on empty, with no internal alarm to warn him. *The safeguard:* **manage it at the system level and report it without penalty.** Roster to limit consecutive long/night shifts, build in real rest, and — critically — create a culture where reporting "I'm too tired to sign for this safely" is *respected, not punished.* On a flight-critical task, a tired person handing it off is a safeguard working, not a failure. Self-vigilance can't be the only defense, because fatigue defeats self-vigilance.

> **STOP & THINK**
>
> The common thread: **self-awareness is not a reliable safeguard for either of these.** The complacent feel sharp; the fatigued feel fine. That's why the real defenses are *external and systemic* — expect-to-find-a-fault inspection discipline and checklists for complacency; rostering and blame-free fatigue reporting for fatigue. Any safeguard that boils down to "just be more careful / just push through" is no safeguard at all. Design the defense outside the tired, confident head.

This connects straight to the AQ-083 message: error is normal, and the human at the sharp end has real limits. Complacency and fatigue are two of the most reliable *performance-shaping factors* in the whole model. You don't fix them by hiring better people — you design work and rosters and inspection routines that hold up even when a good person is over-confident or exhausted.

### 8. Industry example

After a fatigue-linked miss, Dana didn't discipline the technician — she audited the *roster and the reporting culture.* AeroMach had a quiet norm that asking off a flight-critical task for tiredness looked weak. Dana flipped it: handing off a critical task because you're too fatigued to sign for it safely is now logged as a *good catch*, and the rosters were rebuilt to cap consecutive long shifts. Separately, the inspection team adopted an "assume there's a defect" briefing before flight-critical sign-offs. Two precursors that self-awareness can't fix, defended by two systemic safeguards. Audit the process, not the person — *especially* here.

## Lesson 2.2 — Stress & Lack of Resources

*Video + reading · ~9 min · Tunnel vision and the missing tool*

**LAYER B — TEACH THE SUBJECT**

### 7. The teach-out

One of these precursors is inside the person; the other is in the environment. Both end the same way — a corner gets cut — but the safeguards are different.

**Stress.** *Define it:* the strain — from work overload, time demands, or life pressures outside the job — that narrows your attention and degrades judgement. *Why it bites:* under stress the mind goes into *tunnel vision* — you fixate on one thing and stop taking in peripheral information, exactly when you need the wide view most. Stress also feeds irritability and shortcuts. And like fatigue, it's hard to self-assess in the moment; you just feel like you're "powering through." *How it shows up at AeroMach:* a technician dealing with a serious problem at home is mechanically present but mentally narrowed, and misses a cue he'd normally catch. *Consequence:* a normally-sharp person operating with a shrunk field of attention. *The safeguard:* **recognize the signs and relieve the load — pause, talk, get support, and don't carry safety-critical decisions while badly stressed.** Look out for it in colleagues, too. A culture where someone can say "I'm not in the right headspace for the critical job today" — and be reassigned without stigma — is a real safeguard. So is a manager who notices and quietly redistributes the load. Stress isn't a weakness to hide; it's a condition to manage.

**Lack of resources.** *Define it:* not having what the job genuinely needs — the correct tool, the right part, the current data, enough qualified people, working equipment. *Why it bites:* when the right resource is missing, a workaround creeps in. People are conscientious — they *want* to get the job done — so they substitute a "close enough" tool, an uncalibrated gauge, a not-quite-right part, or they do a two-person lift alone. The conscientiousness is real; the substitution is the hazard. *How it shows up at AeroMach:* the calibrated torque wrench for a critical joint is out being serviced, and rather than wait, a tech reaches for a "near enough" one. *Consequence:* a critical characteristic set with the wrong tool, undetectable later. *The safeguard:* **get the right resource — and if you can't, stop and escalate, don't improvise on a critical task.** Ensure the correct, calibrated, current resources are available *before* the task; if one's missing, the safeguard is to *not proceed with a substitute* and to raise it so it gets fixed. Reporting "I can't do this safely without the right tool" is the system working. Improvising on a flight-critical task is the precursor doing exactly what it does.

> **PAUSE & APPLY**
>
> Watch how stress and lack of resources *combine* — that's where they're most dangerous. A stressed, time-pressured person who's also missing the right tool is the perfect storm for a workaround: the stress narrows judgement, the missing resource invites the substitute, and the corner gets cut. The safeguard is to break *either* link — relieve the stress *or* get the right resource — and ideally both. Precursors rarely act alone, which is exactly why naming them individually lets you cut the chain at the weakest link.

Back to AQ-083: stress is a classic *performance-shaping factor*, and lack of resources is a *latent organizational condition* — a hole punched not by the technician but by planning, supply, or calibration scheduling. Naming both as Dirty-Dozen precursors makes them something a supervisor can actually hunt for and fix *before* the workaround is born.

### 8. Industry example

When AeroMach traced a torque issue to a substituted wrench, Dana didn't write up the technician — she audited *why the right tool wasn't there.* The calibration schedule had a gap that pulled critical wrenches out of service with no backup, so a conscientious tech under time pressure improvised. The fix: spare calibrated critical tools, a "no substitute on flight-critical" rule with a fast escalation path, and a clear message that stopping for the right resource is rewarded, not penalized. On the stress side, supervisors were trained to spot a struggling colleague and quietly reassign critical work. Two precursors, two systemic safeguards. The person was the last link — the conditions were the cause.
