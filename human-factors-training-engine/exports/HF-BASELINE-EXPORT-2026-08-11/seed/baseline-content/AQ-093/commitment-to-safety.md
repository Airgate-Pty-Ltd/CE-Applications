# Lesson 2.1 — Visible Commitment and Going to the Floor

## 1. Learning objectives
*After this lesson the learner can:*
- Describe what "visible commitment" looks like in observable behaviour.
- Explain why leaders must go to where the work happens, and what they do there.
- Identify the difference between a "safety tour" that builds trust and one that destroys it.

## 7. The teach-out

Let me make "visible commitment" concrete, because it's a phrase people nod at and never act on. **Visible commitment** is safety leadership that other people can *see with their own eyes* — not infer from a memo. It means the leader spends time, attention and political capital on safety where it can be observed: opening the meeting with it, showing up when there's a hazard, personally backing the person who raised a concern, putting safety on the agenda of decisions that aren't "about" safety. If your commitment to safety lives only in documents, it isn't visible, and invisible commitment shapes no one's behaviour.

The single highest-leverage visible behaviour is **going to the floor** — what some traditions call the leadership walk or "going to where the work is done." Define it simply: the leader regularly goes to the place where the actual hazardous work happens, talks to the people doing it, and *listens.* Why does this matter so much? Three reasons. First, you cannot lead a risk you've never seen; the floor is where the real hazards live, not the conference room. Second, being there signals — visibly — that safety is worth the senior person's time. Third, and most important, it's where you find out what's *really* happening, which is almost never what the report says.

But here's the part that separates a leader who builds trust from one who burns it, and it's worth slowing down on. **Going to the floor can go badly wrong.** If the leader shows up, finds a problem, and publicly blames the nearest worker, they've just taught the floor to hide problems whenever a leader is around. The whole value evaporates. The right way to go to the floor: ask, don't tell. "Walk me through how this works." "What gets in your way?" "What's the thing that nearly went wrong recently?" "What would make this safer?" You're there to *learn and to remove obstacles*, not to inspect-and-punish. Audit the process, not the person — and the floor will start telling you the truth.

> **STOP & THINK**
>
> Picture two general managers who both "do safety walks." One walks the floor, spots an operator with a guard removed, and dresses them down in front of their crew. The other walks the floor, sees the same thing, and asks "help me understand — what made it easier to run it with the guard off?" — and discovers the guard jams the machine three times a shift, so *everyone* removes it. Which GM just learned about a systemic hazard, and which one just guaranteed the next operator will put the guard back the second they see a tie walking over? Same walk. Opposite outcome. The difference is entirely in the leader's behaviour.

So the behaviours to take from this lesson are concrete and repeatable: be *seen* spending your time and attention on safety; go to where the dangerous work actually happens; and when you're there, ask and listen rather than catch and blame. Do those three and you'll know more about your real risks than any dashboard could ever tell you.

## 8. Industry example

Dana committed to walking the AeroMach assembly floor every week — and the first few weeks were awkward, because the floor assumed she was there to find fault. The drift habit (finishing inspection paperwork at end of shift) didn't appear when she was watching. So she changed her behaviour: she stopped pointing at problems and started asking Tariq and the operators, "What's the thing that makes your job harder than it should be?" Within a month, Tariq told her something he'd never have written in a report: the inspection station was a bottleneck, so people batched the paperwork to keep the line moving — a real, systemic hazard nobody had owned. Dana didn't discipline anyone. She fixed the bottleneck. And the floor learned that when the GM comes around, *telling her the truth gets your problem solved* — which is the most valuable thing a safety leader can teach. **The walk only works if what you do on it is safe to tell the truth to.**

## 15. Knowledge Check — graded • 4 questions • 80% to pass

**Q1. "Visible commitment" to safety means:**
- A) A strongly worded safety policy
- B) Safety leadership other people can see with their own eyes — time, attention and capital spent on safety where it's observed
- C) An annual safety award
- D) A signed value statement

> **Best answer: B** — If commitment lives only in documents, it's invisible and shapes no behaviour.

**Q2. The main reason a leader should "go to the floor" / go to where the work is done is to:**
- A) Catch people breaking rules
- B) See the real hazards, signal that safety is worth their time, and find out what's actually happening — which the reports rarely capture
- C) Speed up production
- D) Satisfy an audit requirement

> **Best answer: B** — You can't lead a risk you've never seen; the floor is where the truth and the hazards live.

**Q3. A safety walk *destroys* trust when the leader:**
- A) Asks open questions
- B) Listens more than they talk
- C) Finds a problem and publicly blames the nearest worker
- D) Removes an obstacle the workers raised

> **Best answer: C** — Catch-and-blame teaches the floor to hide problems whenever a leader is near. The walk's value evaporates.

**Q4. The most productive question to ask on a safety walk is closest to:**
- A) "Who's responsible for this mess?"
- B) "Why can't you follow the procedure?"
- C) "Help me understand — what makes this harder or riskier than it should be?"
- D) "Did you read the policy?"

> **Best answer: C** — Ask to learn and remove obstacles. That surfaces the systemic hazard; blame buries it.
