# Module 2 — Situational Awareness: The Shared Mental Model

> **Module goal**
>
> Give the learner a working grip on situational awareness — what it is, how teams build it, the warning signs they're losing it, and how to get it back. Land the idea that a *shared* mental model, kept aligned out loud, is what lets a team catch what one person can't.

## Lesson 2.1 — Building, Losing, and Regaining the Picture

### Learning objectives
*After this lesson the learner can:*
- Define situational awareness and the three levels (perceive, understand, project).
- Recognize the red-flag symptoms that a team is losing the picture.
- Name concrete actions to rebuild situational awareness.

### Competency & Bloom
| Competency | Learning outcome | Bloom's level |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Situational awareness | Detect SA loss and act to rebuild it | Understand / Apply |

### The teach-out

Let me define this one carefully, because it's the most important thing a team can lose without realizing it's gone. **Situational awareness** — SA — is knowing what's going on around you well enough to know what's about to happen. It has three levels, and they stack. Level one: **perceive** — you actually notice the relevant cues (the reading, the alarm, the time, the fuel, the tone in someone's voice). Level two: **understand** — you grasp what those cues *mean together* (this reading plus that trend means the process is drifting). Level three: **project** — you can say what happens next if nothing changes (at this drift rate, we'll be out of tolerance in two lots). Real SA is all three. Noticing a number without understanding it is not awareness; understanding the present without projecting forward is not enough to act in time.

Now the dangerous part, and the reason this is a team behaviour and not a personal trait: **you lose situational awareness gradually and silently.** Nobody feels themselves losing the picture — that's the whole problem. So the industry trained people to watch for the *symptoms* instead, because you can see the symptoms even when you can't feel the loss. Learn this list. The red flags that a person or a team is losing SA: **fixation** (everyone locked onto one problem while others grow unwatched); **confusion or a vague unease** that nobody names; **missing or incomplete information** that people work around instead of chasing; **nobody flying the aircraft** — the metaphor for *everyone got pulled into the same task and no one is watching the whole picture*; **breaking your own rules or procedures** to make something work; and **unresolved discrepancies** — two pieces of information that don't agree and nobody reconciles. When you see these, assume the picture is slipping.

> **STOP & THINK**
>
> Of those red flags, two do the most damage on a floor like AeroMach's: **fixation** and **unresolved discrepancies.** Fixation is the whole team crowded around the one jammed machine while three other jobs drift unattended. An unresolved discrepancy is "the traveler says heat-treated but the tag says not yet" — and everyone's too busy to reconcile it, so the part moves with the contradiction unresolved. Both are SA bleeding out quietly. Name them out loud the moment you see them.

So what do you *do* when you catch the symptoms? You rebuild the picture deliberately. Concrete moves: **say it out loud** — "I think we're losing the picture here, let's reset"; **go back to the facts** — what do we actually know, what are we assuming; **chase the missing information** instead of working around it; **reconcile the discrepancy** before proceeding; and **make sure someone is watching the whole**, not just the hot problem. And the team-level version of all this: keep a **shared mental model.** A shared mental model is everyone on the team holding the *same* picture of the situation — same understanding of the plan, the state, and the risks. Teams keep it shared by saying the picture out loud and updating each other as things change. The moment one person's picture silently diverges from the rest, the team has a hole in it, and the next error walks straight through that hole.

### Industry example

AeroMach had a near-escape that was pure SA loss. A machine alarmed during a run, and Dana, Priya, and two operators all crowded around it — classic fixation. While they did, a second job that needed a time-sensitive process step sat past its window, unattended. Nobody was "flying the aircraft" — watching the whole cell. And there was an unresolved discrepancy nobody chased: the second job's traveler didn't match its tag. Three red flags at once. The save came from Sam walking through, noticing the *symptoms* — everyone clustered on one machine, a job sitting idle, a tag that didn't match — and saying out loud, "Stop, we're losing the picture, let's reset." That sentence — naming the SA loss — is the single most useful thing a leader can say. It rebuilt the shared model before the second job became an escape.

> Recall a time your team got fixated on one problem while something else slipped. Which red flag would have caught it earliest? Write the sentence you wish someone had said out loud.

### Knowledge Check — graded • 4 questions • 80% to pass

**Q1. Situational awareness, fully defined, includes:**
- A) Only noticing cues
- B) Perceiving the cues, understanding what they mean together, *and* projecting what happens next
- C) Reacting fast to whatever happens
- D) Following the procedure exactly

> **Best answer: B** — Perceive, understand, project. Noticing without understanding-and-projecting isn't real awareness.

**Q2. A key danger of losing situational awareness is that:**
- A) It announces itself loudly
- B) It happens gradually and silently — you don't feel it go, so you watch for symptoms instead
- C) It only affects junior people
- D) It can't be regained

> **Best answer: B** — You can't feel SA draining away, so you train on the red-flag symptoms you *can* see.

**Q3. Which is a classic red flag that a team is losing the picture?**
- A) People reconciling discrepancies before proceeding
- B) Fixation on one problem while others grow unwatched, with nobody minding the whole
- C) Saying the situation out loud and updating each other
- D) Chasing down missing information

> **Best answer: B** — Fixation with no one watching the whole ("nobody flying the aircraft") is a textbook SA-loss symptom. The others are *recovery* behaviours.

**Q4. A "shared mental model" is:**
- A) Everyone agreeing with the boss
- B) The whole team holding the same picture of the situation, plan, and risks — kept aligned out loud
- C) A written procedure
- D) One expert's view that others defer to

> **Best answer: B** — A shared model is the team's *common* picture, maintained by speaking it and updating it. The hole appears the moment one person's picture silently diverges.
