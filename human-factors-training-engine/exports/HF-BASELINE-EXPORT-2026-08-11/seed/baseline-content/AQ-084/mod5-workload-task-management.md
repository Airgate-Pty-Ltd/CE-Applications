# Module 5 — Workload and Task Management

> **Module goal**
>
> Teach the human-performance reality that performance collapses at both ends of the workload curve, and the CRM behaviours that manage it: prioritize, distribute, offload, and protect the person who's watching the whole. Land "aviate, navigate, communicate" as a prioritization principle.

## Lesson 5.1 — Managing the Workload Before It Manages You

### Learning objectives
*After this lesson the learner can:*
- Explain why performance degrades under both overload and underload.
- Apply prioritization (the "aviate-navigate-communicate" idea) and task distribution.
- Describe how a team offloads and protects situational awareness when saturated.

### Competency & Bloom
| Competency | Learning outcome | Bloom's level |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Workload management | Prioritize and distribute tasks under load | Understand / Apply |

### The teach-out

Human performance against workload isn't a straight line — it's an upside-down U, and both ends are dangerous. Define the two failure ends. **Overload**: too many tasks, too little time, and the mind starts shedding things — and crucially, it sheds them *without telling you what it dropped.* The first casualty of overload is almost always situational awareness: you get so busy doing tasks that nobody is watching the whole picture. **Underload**: too little to do, long monotony, and attention drifts, complacency sets in, and you miss the one cue that mattered in an hour of nothing. Most CRM attention goes to overload, but underload is the quiet killer on a long, dull shift — the inspector on the four-hundredth identical part who stops really looking.

The CRM behaviours for managing load are practical and trainable. First, **prioritize** — and aviation gives us the cleanest prioritization rule there is: *aviate, navigate, communicate.* Translated out of the cockpit: **first keep the critical thing safe** (don't crash the airplane), *then* handle the navigation/where-are-we-going tasks, *then* the communication and admin. When you're saturated, you protect the top priority and you let the bottom ones wait — you do *not* let a radio call or a form distract you from the thing that's actually keeping people safe. Second, **distribute the workload** — a team has more than one set of hands; the leader who tries to do everything personally creates a single overloaded point of failure while teammates stand idle. Hand tasks out. Third, **offload and automate** the routine so human attention is spent on what matches. Fourth, and most important under saturation: **protect the person watching the whole.** When everyone dives into the hot task, deliberately keep one person out of it, minding the big picture — because that's the exact moment "nobody's flying the aircraft" happens.

> **PAUSE & APPLY**
>
> Aviate-navigate-communicate at AeroMach. A machine faults mid-run, a customer call comes in demanding a status, and a form is due — all at once. Overload. The rule says: **first stabilize the critical thing** (safe the machine, secure the in-process part so nothing gets damaged or mis-processed — that's "aviate"), *then* deal with where this job is going ("navigate"), *then* take the customer call and do the paperwork ("communicate"). Dana's mistake under load would be taking the customer call *first* because it's the loudest — and letting the in-process part get damaged while distracted. Loud is not the same as important. Protect the critical thing first.

*Why does this exist as a trained behaviour?* Because under real load, people instinctively grab the *loudest* or *easiest* task, not the most important one — and they don't notice their SA draining as they do it. *How do you prove a team manages workload?* You see leaders distributing tasks instead of hoarding them, you see someone deliberately kept free to watch the whole, and you see the team protect the top priority when it gets loud. *What happens if it fails?* Under overload, the dropped task is the one nobody chose to drop — and it's often the safety-critical one. Under underload, the missed cue is the one that mattered. Either way the team finds out afterward.

### Industry example

AeroMach's workload lesson came from a chaotic end-of-shift. Three jobs hit trouble at once, the phone rang, and Dana — trying to be everywhere — took the customer call personally while the team scattered across the three problems with no one watching the whole. An in-process part on a fourth job, untouched in the rush, missed its time-sensitive step. Classic overload: the dropped task was the one nobody consciously chose to drop, and SA was the first casualty. The fix Sam coached afterward was pure CRM: under load, *aviate-navigate-communicate* — stabilize the critical work first, distribute the problems explicitly ("Priya takes machine two, I'll cover the picture"), and keep one person — the lead — out of the weeds and watching the whole cell. Next time the shift got loud, nobody had to do everything, and nothing got silently dropped.

### Reflection
> Recall your last truly overloaded shift. What got dropped — and did anyone *choose* to drop it, or did it just fall? What would "protect the person watching the whole" have looked like?

### Knowledge Check — graded • 4 questions • 80% to pass

**Q1. Human performance against workload is shaped like:**
- A) A straight upward line — more load, better performance
- B) An upside-down U — performance degrades under *both* overload and underload
- C) A flat line — load doesn't matter
- D) A downward line only at high load

> **Best answer: B** — Both ends are dangerous: overload sheds tasks silently; underload breeds complacency.

**Q2. Under overload, the first thing a team usually loses is:**
- A) Technical skill
- B) Situational awareness — everyone gets busy doing tasks and nobody watches the whole picture
- C) The ability to follow procedures
- D) Their tools

> **Best answer: B** — SA is the first casualty of overload, and you don't notice it draining while you're busy.

**Q3. "Aviate, navigate, communicate" is a prioritization rule that says:**
- A) Always answer the radio first
- B) Keep the critical/safety thing stable first, then handle navigation, then communication and admin
- C) Do the easiest task first
- D) Do whatever is loudest

> **Best answer: B** — Protect the critical thing first; let the lower priorities wait. Loud is not the same as important.

**Q4. The most important workload behaviour when the whole team gets saturated is to:**
- A) Have the leader personally do every task
- B) Deliberately keep one person out of the hot task, watching the whole, so "nobody's flying the aircraft" doesn't happen
- C) Stop communicating to save time
- D) Take the loudest task first

> **Best answer: B** — Protect the person minding the big picture; that's exactly when SA gets lost.
