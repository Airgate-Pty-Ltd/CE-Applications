## Lesson 7.1 — Error, At-Risk, and Reckless — Drawing the Line Fairly

### Learning objectives

*After this lesson the learner can:*
- Distinguish honest error, at-risk behavior, and reckless behavior.
- State the fair response to each: console/fix the system, coach/remove the reason, hold accountable.
- Explain why mis-sorting these behaviors destroys either reporting or safety.

### The teach-out

In Section 6 I kept promising that honest reporting is safe. Now I have to make good on it, because if reporting *isn't* safe, none of this works — people will go quiet and the heartbeat stops. The thing that makes reporting safe is called **Just Culture**, and it rests on one crucial distinction: **not all unsafe behavior is the same, and it shouldn't be treated the same.** Just Culture sorts behavior into three kinds, and matches a fair response to each.

**Honest error.** *Define it:* a normal human slip by someone who was trying to do the right thing. They didn't intend it, they didn't see it coming, and frankly most competent people would have made the same slip in the same circumstances. *The fair response:* **console the person and fix the system.** You don't punish someone for being human. You ask the Section 2 question — what in the system made this slip likely? — and you fix *that.* Punishing honest error is the single fastest way to kill reporting.

**At-risk behavior.** *Define it:* drifting into a shortcut or a risky habit *without fully grasping the risk.* This is the dangerous middle ground — the person isn't malicious, they've just normalized something risky, often because the system rewarded the shortcut (it was faster, everyone did it, nobody got hurt yet). *The fair response:* **coach the person and remove the reason for the drift.** You help them see the risk they'd stopped seeing, and — just as important — you fix whatever made the shortcut attractive. Punishment misses the point here too, because the drift was systemic.

**Reckless behavior.** *Define it:* *consciously disregarding a substantial and known risk.* This is the rare one — the person knew the risk was serious, knew it was real, and chose to gamble anyway. *The fair response:* **this is where accountability is appropriate.** Just Culture is not soft on recklessness. Someone who knowingly gambles with safety is treated differently from someone who made an honest slip, and that's by design.

> **STOP & THINK**
>
> Here's *why* this three-way sort matters so much, and it cuts both ways. **If you treat honest errors like recklessness** — punish people for being human — they stop reporting, the hazards go underground, and you lose your early-warning system. **If you treat recklessness like honest error** — shrug off someone who knowingly gambled with safety — you tolerate genuine danger and signal that the rules don't matter. Just Culture's whole job is to **draw the line fairly** between these, so reporting stays safe *and* the bar stays high. Get the line right and you get both. Get it wrong in either direction and you lose one of them.

The practical skill for you at awareness level isn't to *judge* anyone — that's not your job. It's to *understand the distinction* well enough that you trust the system. When you grasp that an honest mistake will be met with "let's fix the system," not "you're in trouble," you can report honestly. That trust is the entire point. Just Culture isn't there to let people off the hook — it's there so that the ninety-nine percent of events that are honest errors and at-risk drift get *reported and learned from*, instead of hidden.

### Industry example

Take three AeroMach moments and sort them. **Honest error:** Sofia, new on the job, mis-reads an ambiguous drawing note and builds to the wrong interpretation. She was trying to do it right; the *system* (the ambiguous note) set her up. Response: console her, fix the drawing. **At-risk behavior:** the operator who used the overdue torque wrench because "it's probably fine" and everyone had quietly been doing it. He didn't grasp the real risk; the drift was normalized. Response: coach him, and fix the tool-control system that let an overdue tool stay in service. **Reckless behavior:** *if* someone had known the tool was out of calibration on a flight-critical joint, known it was a serious risk, and used it anyway to hit the schedule — that's a conscious gamble with a known, substantial risk. Response: accountability. Notice that two of the three point straight back at the *system* — which is exactly why Just Culture and "audit the process, not the person" are the same philosophy.

### Reflection

Think of one mistake from your own experience (no names). Was it honest error, at-risk behavior, or reckless? Which response would have actually made things safer — and is that the response it got?

- B) Console the person and fix the system that made the slip likely

- D) It replaces the need to report

> **Best answer: B** — The fair line keeps reporting safe *and* keeps the standard high. Mis-sorting in either direction loses one of them.

## Lesson 7.2 — What Just Culture Protects — and What It Doesn't

### Learning objectives

*After this lesson the learner can:*
- Explain that Just Culture is not a blame-free free-for-all.
- State precisely what it protects (honest people who report honestly) and what it doesn't (reckless gambling with safety).
- Connect Just Culture back to reporting: it's what makes the heartbeat safe.

### The teach-out

There's one misunderstanding about Just Culture I have to kill on sight, because it discredits the whole idea when people get it wrong. Just Culture is **not** a blame-free free-for-all where nothing is ever anyone's fault and any behavior is excused in the name of "culture." That caricature gives leaders a reason to reject it — "we can't just let people do whatever they want" — and they're right to reject *that.* But that's not what Just Culture is.

Here's the precise statement. Just Culture **protects honest people who report honestly.** That's who it's for: the operator who says "I think I mis-set that torque," the engineer who flags "I read this drawing two ways," the inspector who admits "I rushed that and I'm not comfortable with it." Those people are doing exactly what the whole system needs, and Just Culture guarantees they can do it without fear. What Just Culture **does not protect** is someone who *knowingly gambles with safety* — the reckless case from the last lesson. The protection has a clear edge, and the edge is conscious disregard of a known, serious risk.

> **STOP & THINK**
>
> Hold both halves at once, because the power is in the balance. Just Culture keeps reporting **safe** (so honest people speak up and hazards surface early) *and* keeps the bar **high** (so reckless behavior still meets accountability). Drop the first half and you have a blame culture that goes blind. Drop the second half and you have a permissive culture that tolerates danger. Real Just Culture refuses to drop either. That refusal — safe *and* high-bar at the same time — is the entire achievement.

So *why does this matter to you* specifically? Because it tells you exactly what you can count on. If you report an honest issue honestly, the system is on your side — that's a guarantee, not a hope. You will not be punished for being a competent human who noticed something or made an understandable slip. The only way to fall outside Just Culture's protection is to *knowingly gamble* with safety — and if you're the kind of person taking an awareness course seriously, that was never you. The takeaway is liberating: **report freely, report honestly, and trust the line.** That trust is what keeps the heartbeat of SMS beating.

### Industry example

At AeroMach, the operator who reports "I think I mis-set that torque on the last assembly" is *exactly* who Just Culture exists to protect — because that one honest report might stop a part from reaching a customer, and you desperately want him to feel safe making it. Punish him and you've taught the whole floor to stay silent the next time someone has a doubt. Protect him and you've bought yourself an early warning you'd never otherwise get. Dana made the rule explicit at AeroMach: *if you report an honest issue, you are doing your job exactly right, and you are safe.* The number of reports tripled in a month — not because there were suddenly more hazards, but because people finally believed it was safe to surface the ones that were always there.

### Reflection

Would you personally feel safe reporting an honest mistake in your organization today? If not, what would have to change? (This is one of the most important honest answers you'll give all course.)
