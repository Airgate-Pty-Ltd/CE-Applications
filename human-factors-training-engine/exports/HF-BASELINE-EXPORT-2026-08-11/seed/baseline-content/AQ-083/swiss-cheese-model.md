# Section 4 — The Swiss-Cheese Model: Latent and Active Failures

> **Section goal**
>
> Move the learner from "the person at the sharp end" to the whole system. The Swiss-Cheese model shows that a front-line error is almost never the whole story — it had to pass through holes in multiple defences, many of them created long before, far from the floor. The learner leaves able to distinguish active from latent failures and to stop the investigation hunting only the last person.

**LAYER B — TEACH THE SUBJECT**

### 7. The teach-out

Everything so far has helped you understand the *person's* error. Now I'm going to widen the lens, because the most important lesson in human factors is this: **a single front-line error almost never causes a serious failure on its own.** Safe operations have *multiple layers of defence* — procedures, checks, inspections, training, supervision, design safeguards. For a bad outcome to actually reach a person or an aircraft, the error has to slip through *all* of them. The model that captures this is the one I want you to carry out of this course alongside SHELL.

Picture each layer of defence as a slice of Swiss cheese — solid protection, but with holes in it. The holes are the weaknesses, the gaps, the moments a defence doesn't work. Normally the holes don't line up: an error gets through one layer but is caught by the next. A serious accident happens only on the rare occasion when **the holes in every layer momentarily line up** and the hazard passes clean through all of them. That's the **Swiss-Cheese model**, and it reframes your entire approach to failure.

Here's the distinction that makes the model powerful. The holes come in two kinds.

**Active failures** are the errors and violations committed at the **sharp end** — by the person in direct contact with the work, at the moment of the event. The skipped step, the wrong-hand part, the misread value. Active failures are *visible*, they're *immediate*, and they're the ones everybody rushes to. They are the *last* hole the hazard passes through — which is exactly why fixating on them is a trap.

**Latent failures** are the weaknesses built into the **system long before, far from the sharp end** — by decisions made upstream. An unrealistic procedure. A chronic staffing shortage. A look-alike part design that was never fixed. A training gap. A roster that guarantees fatigue. A culture where nobody speaks up. Latent failures are the *pre-existing holes* — they sit dormant in the system, sometimes for years, waiting for an active failure to line up with them. They were created by people who weren't even present when the error happened: designers, planners, schedulers, managers.

> **STOP & THINK**
>
> This is the whole reason "audit the process, not the person" isn't a slogan — it's the only logically sound way to investigate. If you stop your investigation at the active failure (the person who made the error), you've removed *one* hole, in *one* slice. The latent holes — the unrealistic procedure, the fatigue-guaranteeing roster, the look-alike parts — are all still there, perfectly aligned for the *next* normal person to fall through. Blame the last person and you feel like you've solved it. You haven't. You've left every real hole open and pre-loaded the next event.

So when AeroMach has an event, the active failure tells you *who was standing at the sharp end* — useful, but it's the *last* hole, not the cause. The real safety work is hunting the **latent failures**: which upstream decisions, designs, procedures, and conditions created the holes that this error lined up with? Fix those and you don't just prevent *this* failure — you remove holes that dozens of other potential failures would have needed. That's the leverage of thinking in layers instead of in culprits.

### 8. Industry example

An AeroMach assembly with a missed verification reaches final inspection, gets caught, and is pulled — no in-service consequence, but a near-thing. The blame-instinct investigation: "the technician skipped the step; discipline and retrain him." Done in five minutes, and worthless. Dana runs Swiss-Cheese. **Active failure:** the technician omitted the step (a lapse — he was interrupted). That's the last hole. Now the latent holes: the **procedure** allowed an interruptible single-person sign-off with no place-keeping (an upstream design choice); the **roster** had him on a fatiguing shift pattern (a scheduling decision); the **layout** put a noisy distraction beside the bench (a facilities decision); and the **independent check** that should have been the next slice had been quietly dropped to save time months ago (a management decision). Four latent holes, all created by people who weren't on the floor that night, all lined up behind one normal interruption. Fix the technician and you fix nothing. Fix the four latent holes and you've protected every future technician on that bench. Priya finally gets it: "The person was the *last* hole. The accident was built upstream, long before he ever touched the part."

### 15. Knowledge Check — graded • 4 questions • 80% to pass

**Q1. The Swiss-Cheese model says a serious failure happens when:**
- A) One person makes one error
- B) The holes in multiple layers of defence momentarily line up so a hazard passes through all of them
- C) Equipment fails
- D) A rule is written badly

> **Best answer: B** — It takes aligned holes across multiple defences, not a single error.

**Q2. An active failure is:**
- A) A dormant weakness built into the system years earlier
- B) The error or violation committed at the sharp end, by the person in direct contact with the work, at the time of the event
- C) A management decision
- D) A design flaw

> **Best answer: B** — Active failures are the visible, immediate, sharp-end errors — the *last* hole.

**Q3. A latent failure is:**
- A) The last person's mistake
- B) A weakness built into the system upstream — an unrealistic procedure, a fatiguing roster, a look-alike design, a dropped check — created before the event, often by people not present

> **Best answer: B** — Latent failures are the pre-existing, dormant holes created by upstream decisions.

**Q4. Stopping an investigation at the active failure (blaming the last person) is dangerous because:**
- A) It's too slow
- B) It removes only one hole in one layer and leaves every latent hole open and aligned for the next normal person
- C) It's against the rules
- D) It costs too much

> **Best answer: B** — The latent holes remain, pre-loading the next event. That's why you hunt upstream.
