**LAYER B — TEACH THE SUBJECT**

### 7. The teach-out

We've accepted that error is normal and that failures are built in layers. So what do you actually *do*? You manage error on three lines of defence, and you need all three — because no single line is perfect. Let me define each.

**Prevent — make the error less likely to happen at all.** This is the first line: reduce the chance the error occurs. You do it by attacking the SHELL interfaces and the performance-shaping factors. Make the wrong action physically hard (distinct parts, connectors that only fit one way — mistake-proofing). Make the procedure clear and usable. Manage fatigue, workload, and time pressure. Cut interruptions on critical steps. Prevention is the most efficient line — an error that never happens costs nothing downstream — but you can *never* prevent every error, which is exactly why you can't stop here.

**Capture — catch the error after it happens but before it does harm.** This is the second line, and it's the one organizations chronically under-invest in because it *admits errors will occur*. You build in checks that find the error while it's still recoverable: inspections, independent verification of critical features, read-backs on handover, checklists that force a confirmation, functional tests before release. A capture control assumes the prevention line will sometimes fail — and stands ready to catch what gets through. This is the line that turns a potential in-service failure into a caught-on-the-bench non-event.

**Mitigate — reduce the harm when an error gets through both earlier lines.** This is the third line: assume something will eventually escape, and make the consequence survivable. Design margins and redundancy so a single failure isn't catastrophic. Have a recovery path. Build the system so that one missed step doesn't directly equal one in-service loss. Mitigation is the acceptance that even capture isn't perfect — so the final design shouldn't let any single human error become a disaster.

> **STOP & THINK**
>
> Why all three, and why in that order? Because each line *will* sometimes fail, and a system that relies on only one is fragile. Prevention reduces the *number* of errors. Capture catches the ones prevention missed, before they hurt anyone. Mitigation limits the damage from the rare error that beats both. This is the Swiss-Cheese model turned into a *strategy*: you're deliberately building multiple slices, knowing each has holes, so the holes are very unlikely to ever line up. An organization that only does prevention ("write better procedures and train harder") has one slice — and we know what happens when one slice is all you've got.

The single most common error-management failure I see is over-investing in prevention and neglecting *capture* — because capture means admitting your good people will still err. They will. The mature organization builds the catch in anyway. That independent check on the flight-critical feature isn't an insult to the technician's skill; it's the slice that's there for the night the technician — being human — has a lapse.

### 8. Industry example

After the missed-verification near-miss, AeroMach builds all three lines on that assembly. **Prevent:** they cut interruptions at the bench (no-interrupt zone on critical steps) and fix the fatiguing roster — fewer lapses to begin with. **Capture:** they reinstate the independent verification of the critical feature that had been dropped — so if a lapse *does* happen, the next slice catches it before release. **Mitigate:** engineering confirms the design has enough margin and a downstream functional test that a single missed verification won't translate directly into an in-service failure. Three lines, three slices, holes very unlikely to align. Dana's framing for Priya: "We didn't bet everything on the technician never slipping. We assumed he eventually would — like any human — and built a system that's safe anyway. *That's* error management."

**LAYER B — TEACH THE SUBJECT**

### 7. The teach-out

Let me bring the whole course together with two ideas that turn everything we've learned into how an organization actually runs.

First, **error-tolerant design.** *Define it:* work, tools, and systems designed on the assumption that humans *will* err — so that when an error happens, it's hard to make, easy to catch, and unlikely to cause harm. It's the practical opposite of error-*intolerant* work, where a single normal human slip goes straight through to a serious outcome. You already know its ingredients, because they're this whole course turned into design rules:

- **Make the wrong action hard** — mistake-proofing, parts that only fit one way, distinct marking on look-alikes (attacking the hardware interface).
- **Make the right action obvious and usable** — clear procedures and labelling at the point of use (the software interface).
- **Manage the conditions** — control fatigue, workload, time pressure, interruptions (the performance-shaping factors).
- **Build the catches in** — independent verification of critical features, read-backs, confirmation steps (the capture line).
- **Leave margin** — so a single escaped error doesn't directly equal a loss (the mitigate line).

That's it. Error-tolerant work is just SHELL + performance-shaping factors + prevent/capture/mitigate, applied as *design choices made before the work starts*. You're designing for the human you actually have — finite, fallible, normal — instead of the perfect human who doesn't exist.

Now the part without which none of it works: **just culture.** *Define it:* an organizational climate in which people are *not punished for honest, normal errors* — slips, lapses, and reasonable mistakes — but in which genuine recklessness or willful disregard is still held to account, and everyone understands the line between the two. It is *not* a "blame-free" free-for-all (that would excuse recklessness), and it is *not* a blame culture (that punishes normal error). It's the considered middle: honest error is treated as information; reckless behaviour is treated as accountable.

> **STOP & THINK**
>
> Here is the link that ties just culture to everything else in this course, and it's the most important sentence in it: **everything we've built — Swiss-Cheese analysis, finding latent failures, error management — depends entirely on people telling you the truth about what happened. And people only tell you the truth when telling you the truth doesn't get them punished.** Blame the technician who reports his own lapse, and the next person stops reporting. The errors don't stop — they go *underground*, where you can't see them, can't analyse them, and can't fix the latent holes behind them. **A blame culture doesn't reduce error; it reduces your *knowledge* of error — which makes you less safe while feeling more righteous.** Just culture isn't being soft. It's protecting your single most valuable safety asset: the truth.

That's also the deepest meaning of "**audit the process, not the person**." It's not just kinder — it's the only approach that keeps the information flowing *and* points you at the latent failures that actually caused the event. The blame approach feels like accountability but delivers neither safety nor truth: it stops at the active failure, leaves the latent holes open, and teaches everyone to stop talking. Just culture plus systems thinking is how an organization actually gets safer over time — by treating each honest error as a free lesson about where its defences have holes.

### 8. Industry example

AeroMach's turning point wasn't a new machine — it was the day Dana changed how the organization responds to a reported error. A technician self-reports a lapse he caught himself, before it left his bench. Under the old blame culture, he'd have stayed silent and hoped. Under the new just culture, he reports it, it's treated as information, and the investigation finds — through Swiss-Cheese — three latent holes nobody knew about, now fixed for everyone. Word travels: *reporting an honest error here gets the problem fixed, not your name written up.* Reports go *up*, not because errors increased, but because honesty did — and AeroMach can finally see and fix the latent failures it used to be blind to. The one time discipline *was* warranted — a technician who knowingly bypassed a safety check to save time and hid it — everyone understood the difference, because the line between honest error and reckless violation had been made clear. Priya's final note: "Just culture didn't make us soft. It made us able to *see*. You can't fix the holes you can't find, and you can't find them if everyone's hiding."
