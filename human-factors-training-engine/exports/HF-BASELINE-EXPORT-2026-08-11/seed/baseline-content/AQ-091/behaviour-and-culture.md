# Lesson 3.1 — From Pathological to Generative: The Five Rungs

## 1. Learning objectives
*After this lesson the learner can:*
- Name the five rungs of the culture maturity ladder in order and describe the behavior at each.
- Place an organization on the ladder from how it treats safety information.
- Explain that maturity is read from behavior, not from documentation.

## 2. Competency & Bloom
| Competency | Learning outcome | Bloom's level |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Maturity | Place a culture on the ladder by behavior | Understand / Apply |

## 7. The teach-out

It helps to have a ruler — a way to say not just "their culture is weak" but *how* weak, and what the next rung up looks like. The safety field uses a five-rung **maturity ladder**, and the cleanest way to read it is by one question: **how does the organization treat safety information?** That single question sorts every rung. Let me walk them from the bottom up.

**Rung 1 — Pathological.** The attitude is: *"Why waste our time on safety? Don't get caught."* Safety information is unwelcome; messengers are shot; problems are hidden as long as possible. The organization cares about not being *caught*, not about being safe. This is the dark basement.

**Rung 2 — Reactive.** The attitude is: *"We take safety seriously — after every accident."* Safety gets attention in bursts, but only *after* something goes wrong. There's energy, but it's all backward-looking: investigate, fix that one thing, relax until the next event. The organization is always one accident behind reality.

**Rung 3 — Calculative.** The attitude is: *"We have systems in place to manage all hazards."* This is the by-the-numbers rung: lots of process, lots of data collected, audits done, boxes ticked. It *looks* mature — and it's a real step up — but it can be mechanical, managing safety as a paperwork exercise rather than a living concern. Many compliant-but-still-having-accidents organizations live exactly here. They have the systems; they don't yet have the *culture* breathing through them.

**Rung 4 — Proactive.** The attitude is: *"We work on the problems we still find — before they bite."* Now the organization is using its data to get *ahead* of events: spotting trends, acting on near-misses and weak signals, fixing things that haven't hurt anyone yet. Safety has shifted from reaction to anticipation. The workforce is engaged, not just compliant.

**Rung 5 — Generative.** The attitude is: *"Safety is just how we do business around here."* At the top rung, safety isn't a separate program — it's woven into every decision, owned by everyone, and the organization is *chronically uneasy* in a healthy way: it never assumes it's safe, it actively hunts for the next weak signal, and bad news is welcomed because it's information. This is the rung where culture and operations are the same thing.

> **PAUSE & APPLY**
>
> Read the rungs by the information test: **Pathological** *hides* information. **Reactive** *reacts* to it after the fact. **Calculative** *collects and files* it. **Proactive** *acts ahead* on it. **Generative** *hunts for* it. Notice the climb is one continuous idea: how eagerly does the organization want to hear bad news? At the bottom, bad news gets you punished. At the top, bad news gets you thanked. The whole ladder is the journey from shooting the messenger to *recruiting* the messenger.

Two cautions. First, **maturity is read from behavior, not documentation.** An organization with a beautiful SMS manual can sit at Reactive — paper at the top, behavior near the bottom. Don't grade the binder; grade the floor. Second, **the ladder isn't a one-way escalator.** Cultures slide *down* under sustained pressure — a proactive organization that gets squeezed on cost and schedule for two years can quietly fall back to calculative or reactive. Climbing is deliberate and slow; falling is easy. We'll see exactly that pressure mechanism in the next module.

## 8. Industry example

Dana placed AeroMach honestly. The framed poster and the complete SMS manual *suggested* calculative — they had the systems. But the behavior told the truth: investigations only happened after something visible went wrong, the same issues recurred, and bad news got the messenger interrogated. That's **reactive** at best, with **pathological** streaks wherever the schedule pressure was worst — "don't get caught" thinking creeping in at the edges. So AeroMach was a reactive organization wearing a calculative costume. Dana's job over the next two years was a deliberate climb: first secure a just culture so reporting could start (moving toward genuine calculative), then build the informed and learning muscles to reach proactive. You don't leap from reactive to generative. You climb one rung at a time, and you hold each rung against the gravity that wants to pull you back down.

## 14. Reflection
> Place your own organization or area on the ladder — pathological, reactive, calculative, proactive, generative — based on *behavior*, not the manual. Write the one behavior that most justifies your placement.

## 15. Knowledge Check — graded • 4 questions • 80% to pass

**Q1. The five rungs of the culture maturity ladder, from lowest to highest, are:**
- A) Reactive, pathological, calculative, generative, proactive
- B) Pathological, reactive, calculative, proactive, generative
- C) Generative, proactive, calculative, reactive, pathological
- D) Calculative, reactive, proactive, pathological, generative

> **Best answer: B** — Pathological → reactive → calculative → proactive → generative. The climb tracks how eagerly the organization wants to hear bad news.

**Q2. An organization that takes safety seriously *only after an accident* is at which rung?**
- A) Generative
- B) Reactive — attention comes in bursts, always one accident behind reality
- C) Proactive
- D) Pathological

> **Best answer: B** — Reactive is backward-looking energy: fix the last event, relax until the next one.

**Q3. A "calculative" organization with lots of systems and data can still have accidents because:**
- A) It has no procedures
- B) Having the systems isn't the same as having the culture breathing through them — it can manage safety as a mechanical paperwork exercise
- C) It is the highest rung
- D) Data always prevents accidents

> **Best answer: B** — Calculative is a real step up but can be box-ticking. This is where many "compliant but still having accidents" organizations live.

**Q4. Culture maturity should be judged from:**
- A) The quality of the SMS manual
- B) Actual behavior on the floor — a great manual can sit on top of reactive behavior
- C) The number of policies
- D) Whether the organization is certified

> **Best answer: B** — Grade the floor, not the binder. And remember the ladder slides *down* under sustained pressure; climbing is the hard direction.
