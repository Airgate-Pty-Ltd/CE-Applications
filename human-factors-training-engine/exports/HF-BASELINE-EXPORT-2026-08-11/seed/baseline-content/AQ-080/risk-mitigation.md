# Section 5 — Risk Control & Mitigation: The Hierarchy of Controls

> **Section goal**
>
> Teach the learner to reduce risk with the strongest controls first, to verify a control actually moves the risk, and to avoid the trap that defines mature SRM: a control that creates a new hazard. They leave able to design a mitigation and re-assess it.

## Lesson 5.1 — The Hierarchy of Controls: Strongest First

### Learning Objectives
*After this lesson the learner can:*
- Rank controls by the hierarchy: eliminate, substitute, engineer, administrative, PPE.
- Explain why "be more careful" is the weakest possible control.
- Verify a control by re-assessing the risk after it's applied.

### Competency & Bloom
| Competency | Learning outcome | Bloom's level |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Risk control | Apply the hierarchy of controls and re-assess | Apply |

### The Teach-Out

If the risk isn't acceptable, you control it — and *how* you control it matters enormously. There's an order of strength called the **hierarchy of controls**, and the rule is simple: **start at the top and work down; the higher the control, the more reliable it is because it depends less on people behaving perfectly.** Define each, strongest first:

1. **Eliminate** — remove the hazard entirely. No hazard, no risk. If you can design the pinch point out of existence, you never have to manage it again. This is always the first question: *can we just get rid of it?*
2. **Substitute** — replace the hazard with something less dangerous. A safer process, a less hazardous material, a different method.
3. **Engineering controls** — physical controls that don't rely on a person remembering: a guard, an interlock, a barrier, a light curtain that stops the machine if a hand crosses it.
4. **Administrative controls** — procedures, training, signage, supervision, rules. These work *only* as long as people follow them every time — which is why they're weaker.
5. **PPE** — personal protective equipment, the last line: gloves, guards on the person rather than the hazard. PPE protects the individual *if* worn correctly *every* time, and it does nothing about the hazard itself.

Here's the principle I want you to carry: **the strongest controls remove the dependence on human perfection.** Elimination and engineering keep working on the worst day, when someone's tired, rushed, or new. Administrative controls and PPE are only as good as the weakest moment of the most distracted person. That's why **"be more careful" is the weakest control there is** — it's an administrative control with no teeth, asking humans to be flawless forever. Real SRM designs the hazard *out* before it writes a procedure *around* it.

> **STOP & THINK**
>
> The reflex to resist: when a hazard appears, the cheap, fast move is always to write a procedure or issue PPE — bottom of the hierarchy — and call it done. It feels like action. But you've left the hazard fully in place and bet safety on people never slipping. Always climb the hierarchy first: *can we eliminate it? substitute it? engineer it out?* Drop to administrative/PPE only when the stronger controls genuinely aren't practicable — and even then, usually *in addition to*, not *instead of*.

And the discipline that proves a control is real: **after you apply a control, re-assess the risk.** Re-run likelihood and severity *with the control in place* and see if the risk index actually moved. If you "controlled" a red and it's still red, the control isn't working — back to the hierarchy. A control you never re-assessed is a control you only *hope* is working. *How do you prove control is done well?* Controls selected from the top of the hierarchy where practicable, a documented re-assessment showing the risk reduced, and a record of why weaker controls were accepted if stronger ones weren't used. *What if it fails?* You paper the hazard over with procedures and PPE, the risk stays where it was, and the first tired operator on a bad day finds out.

### Industry Example

AeroMach climbs the hierarchy on the pinch point instead of reaching for the bottom. **Eliminate?** They look hard at whether the re-seat-by-hand step can be designed out — and partly it can: a redesigned fixture seats the part reliably so the manual re-seat is rarely needed. **Engineer?** They fit an interlocked guard and a light curtain that stops the clamp if anything crosses the plane — a physical control that doesn't depend on the operator remembering. **Administrative + PPE?** A revised procedure and training back those up — but crucially, *as support, not as the primary control.* Contrast that with the weekend instinct that nearly won the day: "tell them to keep their hands clear." That's "be more careful" — bottom of the hierarchy, hazard fully intact. Then Marcus re-assesses: with the guard and interlock in, likelihood drops from *occasional* to *improbable* and the risk moves out of the red. *Now* the control is real — proven, not hoped.

### Reflection
> Pick a control in your area. Where does it sit on the hierarchy — elimination, engineering, or just "follow the procedure / wear the PPE"? Could you climb higher?

### Knowledge Check — graded • 4 questions • 80% to pass

**Q1. The hierarchy of controls, strongest to weakest, is:**
- A) PPE, administrative, engineering, substitute, eliminate
- B) Eliminate, substitute, engineering, administrative, PPE
- C) Procedure, training, guard, eliminate, PPE
- D) Whatever is cheapest first

> **Best answer: B** — Strongest first: eliminate → substitute → engineering → administrative → PPE.

**Q2. The strongest controls are strongest because they:**
- A) Are the cheapest
- B) Remove the dependence on people behaving perfectly — they keep working on the worst day
- C) Require the most training
- D) Are easiest to document

> **Best answer: B** — Elimination and engineering don't rely on human perfection; admin and PPE do.

**Q3. "Tell them to be more careful" is:**
- A) An engineering control
- B) The weakest kind of control — an administrative measure with no teeth, asking people to be flawless forever
- C) Elimination
- D) Substitution

> **Best answer: B** — It leaves the hazard fully in place and bets safety on human perfection.

**Q4. After applying a control you must:**
- A) Close the assessment immediately
- B) Re-assess the risk with the control in place to confirm the risk index actually moved
- C) Assume it worked
- D) Increase the severity

> **Best answer: B** — Re-assessment is how you prove the control works rather than just hoping it does.

## Lesson 5.2 — Don't Let the Cure Become the Disease: Controls That Create New Hazards

### Learning Objectives
*After this lesson the learner can:*
- Explain how a control can introduce a new hazard.
- Re-run hazard ID and analysis on the *controlled* system, not just the original.
- Spot common "cure becomes the disease" patterns.

### Competency & Bloom
| Competency | Learning outcome | Bloom's level |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Control integrity | Identify hazards introduced by a control | Apply / Analyse |

### The Teach-Out

Here's the trap that separates a practitioner from a beginner: **a control can create a new hazard.** You solve the problem you were looking at and quietly introduce one you weren't. This is so common it has a name in safety circles — *risk migration* — and the discipline against it is one sentence: **after you control a hazard, run hazard identification and analysis again on the changed system, not just the original one.** A control changes the system. A changed system can have new hazards. So you re-look.

Let me make it concrete with the patterns you'll actually meet. You add a guard that blocks the pinch point — but now the operator has to remove it to clear a jam, and the removal step is more dangerous and more frequent than the original hazard. You substitute a less toxic solvent — but it's more flammable, and now you've traded a health hazard for a fire hazard. You add a procedure step that's so slow operators bypass it — and the bypass is the new hazard. You install an interlock that nuisance-trips, so someone defeats it — and now there's no protection at all *and* a defeated safety device. In every case the "cure" was real, but it moved the risk somewhere new, and if you didn't re-look, you don't know it's there.

> **STOP & THINK**
>
> The honest question after every control: *"What did this control just change, and what new hazard could that change introduce?"* Especially watch for controls that are slow, annoying, or in the way — because the human response to a control that fights the work is to defeat it, and a defeated control is worse than none (you've got the original hazard *plus* a false sense of safety). A good control fits the work; a control people have to fight is a new hazard with a delivery date.

*How do you prove you guarded against this?* Evidence that hazard ID was re-run on the controlled system, and that the control was checked for the new hazards it could introduce — including whether people can or will defeat it. *What happens if you skip it?* You close the assessment proud of your fix, the fix creates a quieter hazard, and the next accident comes from the control you were congratulating yourself on. The cure became the disease, and nobody re-looked.

### Industry Example

AeroMach's interlocked guard is a strong control — but Marcus deliberately re-runs hazard ID on the *new* setup and catches two migrated risks. First: to clear an occasional jam, an operator would be tempted to reach around the guard rather than power down — so they add a lockout step and make powering-down faster than reaching around, removing the incentive to defeat the guard. Second: the light curtain nuisance-trips on swarf, and a nuisance-tripping safety device is a device someone eventually bypasses — so they adjust its sensitivity and position before it trains the operators to defeat it. Neither new hazard existed before the control; both were *created* by it; both would have been invisible if Marcus had closed the assessment at "guard fitted, done." The control isn't finished when it's installed — it's finished when you've checked it didn't hand you a new problem.

### Reflection
> Think of a control in your area that's slow or annoying. Is it ever bypassed or defeated? If so, the control didn't reduce the risk — it moved it. What would a control that *fits* the work look like?

### Knowledge Check — graded • 3 questions • 80% to pass

**Q1. After applying a control, you should re-run hazard identification because:**
- A) It's a formality
- B) A control changes the system, and a changed system can contain new hazards the control introduced
- C) The original hazard might have moved on its own
- D) You never re-look once a control is in

> **Best answer: B** — Controls can migrate risk; re-look at the *controlled* system, not just the original.

**Q2. A control that is slow, annoying, or in the way is dangerous because:**
- A) It's too strong
- B) People tend to defeat or bypass it — and a defeated control is worse than none, leaving the hazard plus a false sense of safety
- C) It eliminates the hazard too well
- D) It's always cheaper

> **Best answer: B** — A control people fight becomes a new hazard; good controls fit the work.

**Q3. "The cure becomes the disease" describes:**
- A) A hazard that fixes itself
- B) A control that solves one hazard while introducing a new one (risk migration)
- C) A risk that was never real
- D) PPE working correctly

> **Best answer: B** — Risk migration: the fix is real but it moves the risk somewhere new if you don't re-look.
