# Section 3 — Risk Analysis: Likelihood and Severity

> **Section goal**
>
> Teach the learner to analyse a hazard's risk by judging likelihood and severity honestly and consistently, and to understand what the risk matrix is for (and what it can't do). They leave able to score a hazard the same way twice and resist the pressure to shade severity down.

## Lesson 3.1 — Likelihood × Severity, Judged Honestly

### Learning Objectives
*After this lesson the learner can:*
- Define risk as the combination of likelihood and severity.
- Judge likelihood and severity for a hazard using defined criteria, consistently.
- Explain why severity is anchored to the *credible worst* outcome and never shaded down for convenience.

### Competency & Bloom
| Competency | Learning outcome | Bloom's level |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Risk analysis | Assess likelihood and severity consistently and honestly | Apply |

### The Teach-Out

Define risk before we score it. **Risk is the combination of how *likely* harm is and how *severe* it would be.** Two questions, honestly answered: *how probable is it that this hazard causes harm?* and *if it does, how bad?* Neither question alone is risk. A hazard that's almost certain but trivial (a paper cut) and a hazard that's rare but catastrophic (a flight-critical fastener failure) are completely different risks, and you can only tell them apart by holding both dimensions at once.

**Likelihood** is your judgement of probability — using defined bands like *frequent, occasional, remote, improbable.* The discipline here is to anchor your judgement in evidence where you have it (how often does this condition occur? how exposed are people?) and in honest estimation where you don't. The trap is optimism: "that'll never happen" is not an analysis, it's a hope. Ask instead: *given how this task is really done, how often does someone get exposed to this hazard?*

**Severity** is your judgement of how bad the harm would be *if the hazard is realised* — bands like *catastrophic, hazardous/major, minor, negligible.* And here's the rule I want burned in: **you anchor severity to the credible worst-case outcome, and you never shade it down because the fix is inconvenient.** The most common way risk assessments lie is by quietly rating severity as "minor" because rating it "catastrophic" would force an expensive control. That's not analysis — that's the answer working backward from the budget. If a realised pinch-point hazard can credibly take a hand, the severity is what a lost hand is, full stop. The cost of the control is a *different* conversation, and it happens later, openly, in the acceptance step — never by fudging the score.

> **STOP & THINK**
>
> The two cardinal sins of risk analysis. **Sin one: optimism on likelihood** — "it won't happen" instead of "how exposed are people, really?" **Sin two: convenience on severity** — quietly rating it low because the honest rating demands an expensive fix. Both produce a number that *feels* fine and *is* a lie. The whole value of the analysis is that it's honest even when honest is expensive. Audit the process, not the person — but never audit the score down to protect the schedule.

There's also the question of who scores. SRM works best when **the people who actually do the work** are in the room for the analysis — they know the real likelihood because they live the exposure. A risk scored entirely by a manager who's never run the task tends to be optimistic in exactly the wrong places. *How do you prove your analysis is sound?* Defined likelihood and severity criteria, applied consistently, with the basis for each judgement recorded — and the same hazard scoring the same regardless of who picks up the assessment. *What if it fails?* Inconsistent or optimistic scoring means real risks get rated low, slip under the acceptability line, and never get controlled — until the credible worst case stops being hypothetical.

### Industry Example

The AeroMach team sits down to analyse the pinch point with Priya and one of the operators in the room — deliberately, because they live the exposure. **Likelihood:** the fault-clearing reach-in happens several times a shift, both operators are new to the fixture, and there's no guard — so this isn't "improbable," it's *occasional* at least, arguably *frequent*. **Severity:** a hand in an active clamping mechanism is credibly a crush injury — *hazardous/major*, possibly worse. A supervisor under deadline pressure floats "can't we call severity *minor*, it's only happened the once?" — and Marcus stops it cold: severity is *what the harm would be*, not how often it's happened. It's a hand in a clamp. The honest score is high. The cost of fixing it is a real conversation — but it happens at acceptance, in the open, not by quietly editing the number now.

### Reflection
> Think of a risk in your area that's rated "low." Is it genuinely low likelihood *and* low severity — or has severity been quietly shaded down because the fix is expensive?

## Lesson 3.2 — What the Risk Matrix Is For (and What It Isn't)

### Learning Objectives
*After this lesson the learner can:*
- Explain how a risk matrix combines likelihood and severity into a risk index.
- Read the act/manage/accept bands of a matrix.
- Explain why catastrophic severity earns attention even at low likelihood — and why the matrix is an aid, not the decision-maker.

### Competency & Bloom
| Competency | Learning outcome | Bloom's level |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Risk matrix | Use a matrix to derive and band a risk index | Apply |

### The Teach-Out

Now the tool everyone pictures when they hear "risk assessment": the **risk matrix.** Define it simply — a risk matrix is a grid with *likelihood* on one axis and *severity* on the other; where your two judgements meet, you read off a **risk index** and a colour band that tells you what to do. It turns two separate judgements into one combined picture, and it gives everyone in the organisation a common language — "that's a red" means the same thing in machining as it does in maintenance.

The bands typically resolve to three actions, and this is the useful bit: **unacceptable (act / stop) → manage (acceptable with control, monitor) → acceptable (broadly fine).** Red means the risk is intolerable as it stands — you control it before you proceed, or you stop. Amber means it can proceed but only *with* defined controls and active monitoring. Green means it's broadly acceptable. The matrix isn't there to generate a colour for the file — it's there to drive a decision: *act, manage, or accept.*

Here's the single most important property of a good matrix, and the one people fight against: **catastrophic severity earns serious attention even when likelihood is low.** A hazard that's rare but could kill someone does *not* get to hide in the green corner because "it almost never happens." Low-probability, catastrophic-severity risks are exactly the ones that produce accident reports — rare, severe, and dismissed as unlikely right up until the day they aren't. A matrix that lets catastrophic outcomes sit comfortably just because they're improbable is a badly built matrix, or a badly used one.

> **STOP & THINK**
>
> The matrix is an *aid to judgement, not a substitute for it.* It standardises and communicates the decision — it does not make the decision for you. Two failure modes to avoid: treating the colour as gospel and switching your brain off, and gaming the inputs (shading likelihood or severity) to land on the colour you wanted. Garbage in, confident-looking garbage out. The honesty of the analysis (Lesson 3.1) is what makes the matrix worth anything.

*How do you prove you're using it well?* A defined matrix with documented likelihood and severity criteria, applied consistently across the organisation, with the resulting index recorded and tied to a required action. *What happens if it fails?* Either real risks get a comforting green because someone gamed the inputs, or the matrix becomes a colouring exercise nobody acts on — both leave the actual risk exactly where it was.

### Industry Example

AeroMach's matrix runs likelihood (improbable→frequent) against severity (negligible→catastrophic). The pinch point comes in at *occasional* likelihood and *hazardous/major* severity — that lands in the red, *unacceptable as it stands.* Marcus points out something the team needs to internalise: even if they'd argued likelihood down to *remote*, the severity alone keeps it out of the green — a crush injury is not a green-corner risk no matter how rarely it's expected. That's the matrix doing its job: it won't let a severe outcome be waved away as "unlikely." The colour isn't the point — the *action* it forces is: this one gets controlled before that cell runs another shift.

### Reflection
> Find a risk in your register sitting in the "acceptable" band. Is it there because likelihood *and* severity are genuinely low — or because a severe-but-rare risk got parked in the green corner?

- B) Combines likelihood and severity into a risk index and band, giving a common language and driving an action
- C) Measures cost
- D) Is only decorative

> **Best answer: B** — It turns the two judgements into one banded index that drives act/manage/accept.

**Q2. A catastrophic-severity hazard with low likelihood should:**
- A) Sit in the acceptable/green band because it's rare
- B) Still earn serious attention — low-probability, high-severity risks are exactly the ones that produce accidents
- C) Be ignored
- D) Be downgraded automatically

> **Best answer: B** — Severity keeps a rare-but-deadly risk out of the green corner.

**Q3. The risk matrix is best understood as:**
- A) The decision-maker that replaces human judgement
- B) An aid to judgement that standardises and communicates the decision — but only as honest as its inputs
- C) A budgeting tool
- D) A legal requirement to colour boxes

> **Best answer: B** — It aids and communicates judgement; garbage inputs give confident-looking garbage.

**Q4. "Gaming the matrix" means:**
- A) Using it consistently
- B) Shading likelihood or severity to land on a more comfortable colour band
- C) Recording the index
