### Learning objectives

*After this lesson the learner can:*
- Define a near-miss and explain why it's a complete accident story with the ending changed.
- Explain why near-misses are the most under-reported and most valuable reports.
- Write a near-miss report and overcome the "no harm, no need" reflex.

### The teach-out

Let me define the most under-valued report in the whole system. A **near-miss** is an event where harm *almost* happened and didn't — usually by luck, timing, or someone catching it at the last second. The wrong material almost went into the build but someone noticed at the bench. The crate almost fell but landed back on the shelf. The part with the missing cert almost shipped but got pulled at the dock. Nothing got hurt. No damage. Everyone exhales and moves on.

And that exhale is exactly the problem. Here's the way to understand a near-miss that changes how you treat it forever: **a near-miss is a complete accident story with the ending changed at the last minute by luck.** Every link in the chain lined up — the latent condition, the trigger, the unsafe condition — and the *only* thing that stopped the event was chance. Which means a near-miss is handing you the entire blueprint of an accident, for free, with nobody hurt. It's the cheapest, most honest warning the system will ever get. The organizations that chase down near-misses are the ones that don't have the big events, because they're fixing the accident chains *before* luck runs out.

So *why* are near-misses the most under-reported thing on every floor? Because of a single, very human reflex: **"no harm, no need."** Nothing happened, so why file paperwork? Why make a thing of it? Maybe it makes me look careless. That reflex is the enemy, and naming it is half the cure. The truth is the opposite: the near-miss is *more* valuable than most hazard reports, because it's not "this could go wrong someday" — it's "this *just nearly went wrong*, here's exactly how, and we got lucky." You don't get more actionable than that.

> **STOP & THINK**
>
> Ask yourself the honest question: how many near-misses have you personally walked away from this year and never reported? Not because you're careless — because of "no harm, no need." Now flip it: every one of those was a free, fully-formed warning you threw in the bin. A participant doesn't throw them away. The discipline is simple and hard: **when your heart rate spikes and then it's fine — report it.** That spike is the system's smoke alarm, and you're the only one who heard it.

*How do you report one?* The same six-part structure as any report — but in "what you observed," you tell the *almost*: what nearly happened, and what actually stopped it. That "what stopped it" detail is gold, because it tells the system whether you were saved by a real control (good, reinforce it) or by pure luck (urgent, because luck doesn't scale). *And what happens if you don't report it?* The chain stays intact, fully loaded, waiting for the next time luck isn't there. The near-miss you didn't report is the accident you'll investigate later.

### Industry example

Ray caught a flight-critical assembly with the wrong fastener at final inspection — pulled it before it shipped. Classic near-miss: harm almost happened, didn't, because the last defense held. The old AeroMach reflex was "good catch, move on." Nothing filed. But the new, participating Ray filed it: *what almost happened* (wrong fastener nearly shipped on a critical assembly), *what stopped it* (final inspection caught it — a real control, not luck), and his risk read (catastrophic severity if it had escaped). That report did two things a "good catch, move on" never could: it confirmed final inspection was working as a real defense, and — more importantly — it triggered a look upstream at *why* the wrong fastener got that far, which found a bin-labeling problem at the kitting station. One near-miss report closed a hole nobody else had seen. That's why participants don't waste them.

### Reflection

Recall one near-miss from your own work this year — a moment where it almost went wrong and didn't. Write the two key lines: *what almost happened* and *what actually stopped it.* If "what stopped it" was luck and not a real control, that's an urgent one.

### Knowledge Check — graded • 4 questions • 80% to pass

**Q1. A near-miss is best understood as:**
- A) A minor injury
- B) A complete accident story with the ending changed at the last minute, usually by luck
- C) A false alarm not worth reporting
- D) The same as a completed accident

> **Best answer: B** — Every link lined up and only chance stopped the event; it hands you the full accident blueprint for free, with nobody hurt.

**Q2. Near-misses are the most under-reported reports mainly because of:**
- A) They're too complicated to write
- B) The "no harm, no need" reflex — nothing happened, so why file it
- C) They're banned
- D) Only managers may report them

> **Best answer: B** — "No harm, no need" is the human reflex that throws away free warnings. Naming it is half the cure.

**Q3. The most valuable extra detail in a near-miss report is:**
- A) Who was at fault
- B) What actually stopped the event — a real control (reinforce it) or pure luck (urgent, luck doesn't scale)
- C) The exact time of day only
- D) How embarrassed you were

> **Best answer: B** — "What stopped it" tells the system whether a defense held or whether you were just lucky; the second case is urgent.

**Q4. (T/F) Because no one was hurt, a near-miss doesn't really need to be reported.**
- A) True
- B) False

> **Best answer: B (False)** — The near-miss is *more* valuable than most reports: it's "this just nearly went wrong, here's exactly how." The one you don't report is the accident you'll investigate later.
