### Learning objectives

*After this lesson the learner can:*
- State in one sentence what an incident investigation is and what it's for.
- Explain why a blame-driven investigation cannot find the real cause.
- Describe how this course is structured and how the certificate is earned.

### The teach-out

Let me start with the question that decides whether an investigation is worth anything before it even begins: *what is it for?* Because the honest answer in a lot of organizations is "to find out who to blame and close the file" — and an investigation run for *that* purpose is structurally incapable of doing the one thing an investigation is supposed to do.

Here's the definition, before we lean on the term. **An incident investigation is a structured process for finding out what happened in a safety event, why it happened, and what to change so it can't happen again.** Three words in there matter: *structured* (it's a process with steps, not a conversation), *why* (not just what — the causes, not the surface), and *can't happen again* (the whole point is prevention, not record-keeping). The purpose is to **learn and prevent recurrence.** Full stop. Not to assign fault, not to satisfy a manager, not to close a record by the deadline.

Now the foundational idea, and carry it through every section: **the moment an investigation becomes a hunt for who to blame, it loses the ability to find the cause.** This isn't a soft sentiment — it's mechanical. Your single most valuable evidence in any safety event is the honest account of the people who were there. The instant those people understand that telling you the truth gets someone disciplined, they stop telling you the truth. They tell you the minimum, the safe version, the version that protects them. Your investigation now runs on a sanitized story, reaches a comfortable wrong conclusion, and the real cause — the procedure, the design, the roster, the missing check — stays hidden, perfectly set up to produce the next event. **Blame doesn't reduce error. It reduces your *knowledge* of error.** That's why "learn, not blame" is the first thing we teach: not because blame is unkind, but because blame is *blind*.

> **STOP & THINK**
>
> Picture the same event investigated two ways. **Way one:** the investigator opens with "who signed this off?" The technician, knowing where that leads, says "I must have missed it, won't happen again." File closed: "technician error, retrained." **Way two:** the investigator opens with "I'm not looking for who to blame — I'm trying to find what made this easy to miss, so it doesn't catch the next person." Now the technician tells the truth: "I was pulled away mid-job and the procedure gave me nothing to mark my place." That one honest sentence *is* the investigation — it points straight at the real cause. Same event, same person, same facts on the floor. The *only* difference was the purpose the investigator brought into the room. Blame got a closed file and a recurrence. Learning got the truth and a fix. **The purpose you walk in with determines the cause you walk out with.**

Why spend a whole course on the *process*, then, and not just the mindset? Because "learn, not blame" is necessary but nowhere near sufficient. A well-intentioned investigator who doesn't preserve the perishable evidence, can't run a clean interview, doesn't build a timeline, stops the analysis at the first plausible cause, writes a report nobody can follow, or never checks whether the fix worked — that investigator *still* leaves the event able to come back, just with kinder language. The discipline is in the *process*: every step done properly, in order, every time. Regulators across the major aviation authorities expect a *systematic* investigation process precisely because they learned, repeatedly, that good intentions and a blamed individual don't prevent the next accident.

A word on how the course runs. Seven sections. We start here — why we investigate. Then **the trigger and scoping** — what makes an event worth investigating and how deep to go. Then **immediate response and evidence preservation** — the first-hour moves that decide whether you'll have anything to investigate at all. Then **building the timeline and gathering facts** — including interviews done well, which is a craft. Then **analysis** — where we hand off to the RCA methods from AQ-087 and show what a good analysis must deliver. Then **corrective action and the report** — acting at the cause and writing it so it holds. And finally **follow-up and effectiveness** plus the final assessment. Each section ends in a graded check at eighty percent; the course ends with a final exam at eighty percent plus a graded capstone where you run a full investigation end to end.

And the reassurance that pairs with the bar: **if you attend the whole course and engage with AeroMach's events, you should not have a problem.** The bar is high because an investigator who runs a sloppy or blame-driven process is dangerous to an organization — they make it *feel* like the problem was handled while leaving it wide open. Clearing the bar is the point.

### Industry example

At AeroMach, Dana the Safety Manager keeps one story for every new safety officer. Years ago, before she owned the process, three separate events in three years were each closed the same way: "technician error; retrained." Three different people, three "handled" problems. When the *fourth* one landed on her desk, she ran it as a process instead of a verdict — preserved the scene, built the timeline, interviewed for the truth, analysed past the person — and found that all four were the *same* problem: a procedure that allowed a single person to sign off a critical step with no place-keeping, untouched for years because every investigation had stopped at the person. Four investigations had blamed four people and left the actual cause sitting there. Priya, the new officer, is about to learn in a week the discipline it took Dana four recurrences to build. Dana's line: "We didn't have four problems. We had one problem and four bad investigations. The process is what turns one event into one fix — instead of into a person."

### Reflection

**Evaluate your own situation**

Think of one safety event, incident, or near-miss you've seen investigated (yours or one you witnessed). In one line: was the investigation run to *learn* (it ended in a changed condition — a procedure, design, roster, check) or to *blame* (it ended in a named person — "be more careful," retrained, written up)? Keep it — by the end of this course you'll be able to run that same event as a real investigation.

### Knowledge Check — graded • 3 questions • 80% to pass

**Q1. The purpose of an incident investigation is to:**
- A) Find out who to blame and close the file
- B) Learn what happened and why, and change what's needed so the event can't recur
- C) Satisfy the manager by the deadline
- D) Decide on discipline

> **Best answer: B** — An investigation is a structured process for learning and preventing recurrence — not assigning fault.

**Q2. A blame-driven investigation cannot find the real cause mainly because:**
- A) It takes too long
- B) It dries up the honest human accounts — your most valuable evidence — so you reach a confident but false conclusion and miss the real cause
- C) It costs too much
- D) It's against regulations

> **Best answer: B** — Blame reduces your *knowledge* of error, not error itself; people stop telling you the truth.

**Q3. "Learn, not blame" is necessary but not sufficient because:**
- A) Blame is sometimes fine
- B) A well-intentioned investigator who runs a sloppy *process* — no evidence preservation, weak interviews, shallow analysis, no follow-up — still leaves the event able to recur
- C) The mindset is all that matters
- D) Process doesn't matter in safety

> **Best answer: B** — The discipline is in the process; good intentions without disciplined steps still fail to prevent recurrence.
