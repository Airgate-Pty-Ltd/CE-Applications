### Learning objectives

*After this lesson the learner can:*
- Explain why safety communication must be two-way, and what each direction carries.
- Name the common channels for each direction (alerts/boards/briefings vs. the reporting system).
- State the test of whether top-down communication actually "landed."

### The teach-out

Most people, when they hear "safety communication," picture a one-way street: management broadcasts, everyone receives. That picture is **half the system, and the wrong half to lead with.** Let me define it properly. **Safety communication is the two-way movement of safety information through the organisation** — *down* from leadership (what matters, what's changed, what we just learned) and *up* from the floor (hazards, concerns, near-misses, "this didn't feel right"). Both directions are safety-critical, and an organisation that's strong on one and weak on the other has a broken pillar.

Let me take each direction in turn.

**Top-down communication** is how leadership puts safety information *into* people's hands. *What it carries:* the safety policy and objectives, what's changed in procedures, what was learned from a recent event, what the current safety priorities are. *Channels:* safety alerts and bulletins, safety boards (physical and digital), toolbox talks and shift briefings, the safety meeting cascade, newsletters. *Why it exists:* people can't act on information they never received, and they can't prioritise safety they're never told the organisation cares about. *The trap:* confusing *broadcasting* with *communicating*. Sending a bulletin is not the same as the bulletin being read, understood, and acted on.

**Bottom-up communication** is how hazards and concerns travel *from* the floor *to* the system. *What it carries:* hazard reports, near-miss reports, safety concerns, suggestions. *Channels:* the safety reporting system, the open door, the safety meeting where the floor actually speaks, the conversation a supervisor escalates. *Why it exists:* this is the input to Safety Risk Management — and we proved in Module 1 that SRM can only manage what it hears about. **Bottom-up communication is the lifeblood of the SMS.** When it flows, the system has data. When it stops, the system goes blind. This is also why the *culture* section matters so much: bottom-up flow only happens where it feels safe, and we'll build that next.

> **PAUSE & APPLY**
>
> Here's the test for whether your *top-down* communication actually worked, and it's Brian's favourite: **the test isn't "did we send it" — it's "did behaviour change."** A safety bulletin that everyone scrolled past changed nothing. The auditor question is "show me the objective evidence this communication *landed*" — and "landed" means a changed practice, a question raised, a correct answer when you ask someone on the floor what the alert was about. Plain language, real examples, and visible follow-through beat a wall of policy text every time. If you want to know whether your safety board works, don't count the postings — ask three people what's on it.

And the two directions are connected by one thing that makes or breaks the whole pillar: **closing the loop.** When someone reports something (bottom-up) and then *sees the result come back to them* (top-down) — "you reported X, here's what we did" — you've completed the circuit, and you've just taught the entire floor that reporting works. When the loop never closes, bottom-up dies, and no amount of top-down broadcasting revives it. Two-way communication isn't two separate systems; it's one loop, and the loop has to close.

### Industry example

AeroMach was excellent at top-down and terrible at bottom-up — and didn't know it. Dana's team pushed out polished safety bulletins, ran a slick safety board, and held monthly briefings. But when she traced what *came back up*, the reporting system was nearly silent, and the few reports that came in vanished without a reply. The broadcast was loud; the listening was dead. Worse, the top-down itself wasn't landing — when Priya asked operators what last month's headline bulletin had been about, most couldn't say. The fix wasn't a better bulletin; it was closing the loop: every report got an acknowledgement and, when actioned, a visible "here's what we did" that went *onto* the safety board. Within two months, reporting tripled — not because people were told to report, but because they finally saw it work.

### Reflection

Pick the last safety message your organisation pushed out. Could three people on the floor tell you what it was about and what they should do differently? If not, it was broadcast, not communicated — write down how you'd test "did behaviour change" next time.

### Knowledge Check — graded • 4 questions • 80% to pass

**Q1. Safety communication must be two-way because:**
- A) It's a regulatory formality
- B) Top-down carries what matters and what changed; bottom-up carries hazards and concerns into the SMS — and both are safety-critical
- C) Only top-down matters
- D) Only bottom-up matters

> **Best answer: B** — One loop, both directions; weak on either is a broken pillar.

**Q2. Bottom-up communication is "the lifeblood of the SMS" because:**
- A) It's the most-used channel
- B) It's the input to Safety Risk Management — the system can only manage hazards it hears about
- C) It's required by law in all cases
- D) It replaces top-down

> **Best answer: B** — No bottom-up flow, no hazard data, blind SRM.

**Q3. Top-down safety communication has "landed" when:**
- A) The bulletin was sent
- B) Behaviour changed — a practice shifted, a question got raised, people can say what it meant
- C) It was filed
- D) It was long and detailed

> **Best answer: B** — "Did we send it" is not the test; "did behaviour change" is.

**Q4. "Closing the loop" means:**
- A) Ending the safety meeting on time
- B) Feeding the result of a report back to the reporter and the floor — so people see that reporting works
- C) Shutting down a reporting channel
- D) Archiving old reports

> **Best answer: B** — The loop has to close, or bottom-up flow dies.
