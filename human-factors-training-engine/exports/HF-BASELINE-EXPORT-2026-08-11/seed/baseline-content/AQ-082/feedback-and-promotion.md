### Learning objectives

*After this lesson the learner can:*
- Structure a blame-free lesson learned (what happened / real cause / what changed / how we confirm it spread).
- Explain why an unshared event is "a lesson paid for and thrown away."
- Describe how to spread a lesson so other teams actually absorb it.

### The teach-out

Every safety event your organisation has — every near-miss, every error caught, every small failure — is paid for. Somebody's time, somebody's scare, sometimes somebody's money or near-injury bought that information. So here's the question that defines this lesson: **once you've paid for a lesson, do you let one team keep it, or does the whole organisation get to learn it?** An event the rest of the organisation never hears about is, in Brian's phrase, **a lesson paid for and thrown away.** Knowledge sharing is how you stop throwing them away.

Let me define a lesson learned the way it actually has to work, and notice it's built directly on Just Culture from the last module. **A lesson learned is a blame-free account of an event that captures three things and then does a fourth:**

1. **What happened** — the facts, plainly, *without blame*. The moment a lesson learned names-and-shames, two things die: people stop cooperating with the next investigation, and everyone reads it as a hit job rather than a learning tool. *Audit the process, not the person* isn't a slogan here; it's what makes the document usable.
2. **What the real cause was** — not the surface "operator made an error," but the *system* reason underneath: the procedure that didn't match reality, the station that was understaffed, the training gap, the channel nobody trusted. The honest cause is almost always systemic, and the systemic cause is the one worth fixing.
3. **What changed** — the actual fix. A lesson learned with no change attached is just a story. The whole point is that the system is different now.
4. **And then — how you confirm the learning spread.** This is the step everyone skips and the step that separates *organisational* learning from a filed report. It's not enough to write the lesson; you have to get it to the people who could repeat the event and confirm it landed — the same "did behaviour change" test from the communication module.

> **PAUSE & APPLY**
>
> Watch the pillars connect here, because this is the whole course in one move. A near-miss gets *reported* (reporting culture, Module 4) → it's investigated *blame-free* (Just Culture, Module 4) → the real *systemic* cause is found and *fixed* → the lesson is *communicated* to every team that could hit the same hazard (two-way communication, Module 3) → and you *confirm it landed* (did behaviour change). That chain is Safety Promotion doing its actual job: turning one team's bad day into the whole organisation's competence. Break any link — don't report it, blame someone, skip the fix, don't share it — and the lesson is lost.

How do you actually *spread* a lesson so it sticks? Not by filing it in a folder nobody opens. You push it through the channels we built in Module 3: a safety alert if it's urgent, a standing item on the safety board, a slot in the toolbox talk and the safety meeting, a short "here's what happened and what we changed" that's *concrete and human* rather than a sanitised report. The best lessons-learned cultures make sharing routine and easy — and they make it *two-way*, so a team receiving a lesson can come back with "we have that exact risk over here too." *Consequence of getting it wrong:* the same event happens again in the next department over, and the investigation finds that someone, somewhere in the building, already knew — you just never moved the knowledge.

### Industry example

After AeroMach's cracked-part near-miss, Sam wrote it up the right way: what happened (plainly, no names), the real cause (an understaffed inspection station and a reporting channel people didn't trust — *system*, not person), and what changed (staffing fixed, channel rebuilt, the loop now visibly closed). Then came the step that mattered: instead of filing it, Dana turned it into a one-page "what we learned" that went onto every line's safety board and into every toolbox talk for a fortnight — and a second inspection cell, hearing it, said "we've got the same staffing squeeze." A near-miss in one cell *prevented* one in another, because the lesson was shared instead of shelved. Priya's takeaway: "The investigation was the cheap part. The sharing was the part that actually made us safer."

### Reflection

Think of a recent event in your world. Write its lesson learned in four lines: what happened (no blame), the real *systemic* cause, what changed, and how you'd confirm the learning spread. Notice how hard — and how important — line three and four are.

### Knowledge Check — graded • 4 questions • 80% to pass

**Q1. A blame-free lesson learned must capture, at minimum:**
- A) Who was at fault and what discipline followed
- B) What happened (no blame), the real systemic cause, what changed — and how you confirm the learning spread
- C) Only what happened
- D) Only the corrective action

> **Best answer: B** — Facts, real cause, the fix, and confirmation it spread — built on "audit the process, not the person."

**Q2. An event the rest of the organisation never hears about is:**
- A) Efficiently contained
- B) A lesson paid for and thrown away
- C) The ideal outcome
- D) None of the organisation's business

> **Best answer: B** — You already paid for the lesson; sharing is how you stop wasting it.

**Q3. The "real cause" in a lesson learned is almost always:**
- A) The individual who made the error
- B) A systemic reason — a procedure that didn't match reality, an understaffed station, a training gap, an untrusted channel
- C) Bad luck
- D) Irrelevant

> **Best answer: B** — Audit the process; the systemic cause is the one worth fixing.

**Q4. The step that separates *organisational* learning from a filed report is:**
- A) Writing a longer report
- B) Getting the lesson to the people who could repeat the event and confirming it landed (did behaviour change)
- C) Archiving it properly
- D) Assigning blame

> **Best answer: B** — Sharing-and-confirming is what makes it organisational, not local.
