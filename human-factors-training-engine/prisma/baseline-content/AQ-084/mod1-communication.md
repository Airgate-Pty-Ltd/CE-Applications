# Module 1 — Communication: Getting the Knowledge Out of People's Heads

> **Module goal**
>
> Make the learner fluent in the communication behaviours that actually move information reliably between people under pressure: closed-loop communication, advocacy and inquiry, and assertive speaking-up across an authority gradient. They leave able to tell the difference between "I told them" and "they confirmed it" — and to do the second one.

## Lesson 1.1 — Closed-Loop Communication: Say It, Confirm It

### Learning objectives
*After this lesson the learner can:*
- Define closed-loop communication and contrast it with open-loop "I told them."
- Explain why confirmation, not transmission, is the point.
- Apply closed-loop communication to a handoff in their own work.

### Competency & Bloom
| Competency | Learning outcome | Bloom's level |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Communication | Use closed-loop communication on real handoffs | Understand / Apply |

### The teach-out

Here's the most useful communication habit in this entire course, and it's almost embarrassingly simple. Let me define it before the name. **Closed-loop communication** is a three-step loop: I send a message, *you read it back or restate it*, and I confirm you got it right. Three steps — send, receive-and-confirm, confirm-the-confirmation. The loop is **closed** only when the sender has heard the receiver repeat the meaning back. Until that happens, the loop is **open**, and an open loop is just hope.

Contrast it with what most of us actually do all day: **open-loop communication.** I say the thing. I assume you heard it, understood it, and will act on it. I move on. No read-back, no confirmation. The trouble is that "I told them" feels exactly like "they understood me" — right up until the moment it turns out they didn't. The most dangerous four words in any operation are *"I thought you had it."* Two people, each certain the other was handling it, and the thing in the middle falls on the floor. Closed-loop communication is the specific cure for "I thought you had it."

*Why does it exist?* Because under noise, stress, and time pressure — exactly when communication matters most — open-loop failure rates climb. People mishear numbers. They hear what they expected instead of what was said. They acknowledge with "yeah, got it" while thinking about something else. The read-back catches all of that *before* it becomes an action. It's the same reason a critical instruction in a control room gets repeated back, and the same reason a good nurse reads back a drug dose. The read-back isn't bureaucracy or distrust — it's the cheapest error trap ever invented.

> **PAUSE & APPLY**
>
> Closed-loop at AeroMach: Dana tells Priya, *"Hold lot 4471 — it's on the quality clarification."* Open-loop ends there. Closed-loop: Priya says, *"Holding 4471 for quality clarification, not 4417?"* and Dana says, *"Correct — 4471."* That one extra exchange just caught a digit transposition that would have held the wrong lot and shipped the bad one. **Send, restate, confirm.** Build the read-back into every instruction that matters and "I thought you had it" stops happening.

*How do you prove you do it?* You can hear it. In a team that runs closed loops, instructions come back restated, hand-offs are confirmed out loud, and numbers get repeated. In a team that doesn't, you hear a lot of "yep" and "got it" and then a lot of surprises. *What happens if you get it wrong?* The instruction that was never really received becomes the action that was never really taken — or the wrong action entirely. At AeroMach that's a wrong lot shipped; in a cockpit it's a clearance misheard on a busy frequency. Same open loop, different altitude.

### Industry example

One of the classic aviation lessons — told generically, the way the industry teaches it — is a ground collision in poor visibility where one crew believed they had a clearance to proceed and the controllers and another crew believed they did not. The words exchanged were ambiguous, the loop was never properly closed, and each party acted on its own assumption. Hundreds died. The lesson the whole industry took from it was blunt: **ambiguous, unconfirmed communication kills.** Out of that came the discipline of standard phrasing and explicit read-backs — closing the loop, every time, especially when the stakes and the noise are both high. The exact same loop that would have caught that clearance is the one that catches Dana's lot number.

### Reflection
> Pick one handoff you do regularly (a shift change, a disposition, a work order release). Is the loop closed — does someone restate it back to you — or are you running on "I told them"? Write down one place you'd add a read-back.

### Knowledge Check — graded • 3 questions • 80% to pass

**Q1. Closed-loop communication means:**
- A) Saying the instruction loudly once
- B) Send the message, have the receiver restate it, and confirm it was understood correctly
- C) Putting everything in an email
- D) Repeating yourself until people stop arguing

> **Best answer: B** — The loop closes only when the receiver restates the meaning and the sender confirms. Three steps.

**Q2. "I thought you had it" is the classic symptom of:**
- A) Closed-loop communication working
- B) Open-loop communication — each person assumed, no one confirmed
- C) Too much briefing
- D) An authority gradient

> **Best answer: B** — Two people each assumed the other had it; the loop was never closed. That's open-loop failure.

**Q3. The read-back is valuable mainly because:**
- A) It shows respect for rank
- B) It catches mishearings and misunderstandings *before* they become actions, especially under noise and stress
- C) It slows the team down on purpose
- D) It creates a paper trail for blame

> **Best answer: B** — Confirmation traps the error at the cheapest possible moment — before anyone acts on it.

## Lesson 1.2 — Advocacy, Inquiry, and Assertive Speaking-Up

### Learning objectives
*After this lesson the learner can:*
- Define advocacy and inquiry and explain why a team needs both.
- Use a structured, graded way to escalate a concern (the "assertive ladder" idea).
- Explain why stating the concern *and the reason and the recommendation* beats hinting.

### Competency & Bloom
| Competency | Learning outcome | Bloom's level |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Communication | Advocate a concern and inquire to surface others' | Understand / Apply |

### The teach-out

Closed-loop communication moves a *clear* message reliably. But a lot of the knowledge that prevents accidents isn't clear yet — it's a half-formed worry, a "something's not right," a junior person's quiet doubt. Getting *that* onto the table needs two more behaviours. Let me define both before naming them.

**Advocacy** is stating your position and the reason for it, clearly and out loud. Not hinting. Not "is everything okay with that lot?" — but "I'm concerned about lot 4471 because the surface finish reading is outside what I've seen pass, and I think we should hold it." Advocacy is *putting your concern on the table with its reasoning attached.* **Inquiry** is the opposite-facing skill: actively pulling the other person's picture out of them — "What am I missing here?", "What are you seeing that I'm not?", "Walk me through why you're comfortable." A healthy team runs both: people advocate their own concerns, and they inquire to surface everyone else's. A team that only advocates is a shouting match; a team that only inquires never commits. You need both.

Here's why advocacy specifically has to be *explicit*. Under pressure and across a rank difference, people soften concerns into hints — and hints get missed. "Are you sure about that?" is a hint. The other person, busy and senior, hears mild curiosity and moves on. The concern was technically voiced and completely failed to transfer. So the discipline is to escalate in graded steps that get more direct until you get a real response. The plain-language version of the **assertive ladder**: start with a hint or a question; if that doesn't land, state your concern directly; if that doesn't land, state the concern *and* your recommendation; and if the stakes are high enough and you're still being waved off, you make a direct challenge — *"Stop. I am not comfortable proceeding, and here's why."* You climb the ladder only as far as you need to. But you must be willing to climb to the top when the consequence is serious.

> **PAUSE & APPLY**
>
> Watch the ladder fail and then work at AeroMach. **Fails:** Priya says, "Huh, that's an unusual reading" (a hint). Marcus, heads-down, says "mm-hm" and signs it off. Concern voiced, nothing transferred. **Works:** Priya climbs the ladder — "Marcus, I want to flag a concern: this finish reading is outside our pass range, and I recommend we hold the lot until we verify the gauge." That's advocacy with a reason and a recommendation, stated plainly. Now it's on the table and Marcus has to actually respond to it. **The concern only counts if it was transferred — not if it was technically uttered.**

*Why does explicit advocacy matter so much?* Because the entire value of a team is that more than one set of eyes saw the problem. If the second set of eyes saw it but only hinted, you paid for two inspectors and got the safety margin of one. *How do you prove a team does this?* You see people raise concerns with reasons attached, you see leaders ask "what am I missing?", and — the real tell — you see concerns get *welcomed*, not punished. *What happens if it fails?* The doubt stays in someone's head, the part ships, and in the investigation you find the sentence that haunts every one of these cases: *somebody knew.*

### Industry example

The other classic lesson the industry teaches generically is the slow-motion accident: a crew so fixated on one problem — a warning light, a minor malfunction — that nobody clearly advocated the bigger threat that was quietly growing, in some cases the fuel running out while everyone worked the small problem. The information was on the gauges. Someone likely sensed it. But it was hinted, not advocated, and never forced onto the table as *"we have a bigger problem than this light, and we need to deal with it now."* The lesson: a concern that isn't advocated clearly and escalated when ignored is the same, operationally, as a concern nobody ever had. AeroMach's version is quieter and just as real — Priya's reading, hinted and waved off.

### Reflection
> Think of a concern you once softened into a hint because of who was in the room. Rewrite it as proper advocacy: the concern, the reason, and your recommendation, in one or two sentences.

### Knowledge Check — graded • 4 questions • 80% to pass

**Q1. Advocacy means:**
- A) Asking vague questions and hoping someone notices
- B) Stating your position *and the reason for it* clearly and out loud
- C) Staying quiet until you're certain
- D) Deferring to the most senior person

> **Best answer: B** — Advocacy is putting your concern on the table *with its reasoning*, not hinting.

**Q2. Inquiry is the skill of:**
- A) Interrogating people to assign blame
- B) Actively pulling the other person's picture out — "What am I missing? What are you seeing that I'm not?"
- C) Filing a formal report
- D) Repeating your own position louder

> **Best answer: B** — Inquiry surfaces the *other* person's information. A healthy team runs advocacy *and* inquiry.

**Q3. The "assertive ladder" idea says you should:**
- A) Always start with a direct challenge
- B) Escalate in graded steps — hint, then state the concern, then add your recommendation, then a direct challenge — going only as far as needed
- C) Voice a concern exactly once, then drop it
- D) Never challenge someone senior

> **Best answer: B** — Climb in steps until you get a real response — and be willing to reach the top when the stakes are high.

**Q4. A concern that was hinted and waved off is, operationally:**
- A) Fully handled — you did your part
- B) Effectively the same as a concern nobody ever raised, because it didn't transfer
- C) The other person's fault entirely
- D) Proof the team communicates well

> **Best answer: B** — The concern only counts if it was *transferred* and addressed. A hint that didn't land changed nothing.
