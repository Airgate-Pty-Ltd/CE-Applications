# Module 3 — Decision-Making Under Pressure

> **Module goal**
>
> Give the learner a structured, repeatable way to decide under pressure that beats gut reaction, and show why pooling the team's information before committing is the behaviour that catches bad decisions early.

## Lesson 3.1 — A Structured Way to Decide When the Clock Is Running

### Learning objectives
*After this lesson the learner can:*
- Explain why unstructured decisions degrade under stress and time pressure.
- Apply a simple structured decision model (a plain-language DECIDE-style loop).
- Describe why pooling team information before committing is a decision-quality control.

### Competency & Bloom
| Competency | Learning outcome | Bloom's level |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Decision-making | Apply a structured decision model under pressure | Understand / Apply |

### The teach-out

Under pressure, the human decision-making default is to grab the first workable option, commit hard, and stop looking — and then to defend that first choice even as evidence piles up against it. That default served us well on the savanna and serves us badly in a high-consequence operation, because the cost of committing to the wrong option fast can be enormous. So CRM trains a *structured* way to decide, precisely so the structure carries you when stress is eating your judgement. You don't rise to the occasion; you fall back on your training. Give yourself a structure to fall back on.

Let me give you a plain-language version of a structured decision loop — the kind aviation teaches under names like the DECIDE model, taught here by concept so we're not reproducing anyone's copyrighted material. Six moves: **Detect** that something needs a decision (don't drift into a choice — notice you're making one). **Estimate** the significance — how bad, how urgent, how much time do I actually have. **Choose** the desired outcome — what does "good" look like here, what am I trying to achieve. **Identify** the options — and force yourself to name more than one, because the trap is seeing only the first. **Do** — commit and act on the best option. **Evaluate** — and this is the step people skip — check whether it's actually working, and be willing to revise. That last step is the antidote to the human default of defending the first choice. A structured decider stays willing to be wrong.

> **PAUSE & APPLY**
>
> Two failure modes the structure prevents, both deadly. The first is **deciding without realizing you're deciding** — drifting into a course of action because it's the path of least resistance. "Detect" forces you to *notice* the decision point. The second is **tunnel-commitment** — locking onto option one and defending it as evidence mounts against it. "Identify options" and "Evaluate" pry the tunnel open. At AeroMach: Marcus feels time pressure on a borderline part and his gut says "it'll be fine, ship it." The structure makes him stop — *what am I actually deciding, how much time do I really have, what's option two (hold and verify), is shipping actually working out or am I just defending my first instinct?*

Now the team layer, because a structured *individual* decision is good and a structured *team* decision is better. The single most powerful decision-quality behaviour in CRM is **pooling information before committing** — the leader actively asks, "What am I missing? What does everyone see?" *before* locking the choice. Here's why it matters so much: the information that would change the decision is often sitting in the head of the most junior, quietest person — who will not volunteer it unless asked. A leader who decides fast and alone gets the average of one brain. A leader who pools first gets the team. The failure mode is the opposite: one person deciding fast while the team holds its doubt in silence. Your job, whether you lead or follow, is to get the doubt *out* before the commit, not after the consequence.

### Industry example

AeroMach's near-escape on decision-making was a borderline disposition under shipping pressure. Marcus's gut said ship; the clock said now. What saved it was Dana running the team version of the loop out loud: "Before we commit — what does everyone see on this one?" Priya, asked directly, said the thing she'd never have volunteered: the gauge that took the reading had been flagged as due for calibration. That single pooled fact changed the estimate of the situation entirely — the reading itself was now suspect. The decision flipped from "ship" to "hold and re-measure on a verified gauge," and the bad part stayed in the building. Same facts were available before the question; the *question* — pooling before committing — is what pulled them out in time.

### Reflection
> Think of a pressured decision you've made recently. Did you notice the decision point, or drift into it? Did you name a second option? Did you check whether it was working? Write one step you'd add next time.

### Knowledge Check — graded • 4 questions • 80% to pass

**Q1. The human default when deciding under pressure is to:**
- A) Generate many options and weigh them calmly
- B) Grab the first workable option, commit hard, and defend it as contrary evidence appears
- C) Always defer the decision
- D) Pool everyone's information first

> **Best answer: B** — Fast tunnel-commitment is the default the structure is designed to counter.

**Q2. A structured decision model helps under pressure because:**
- A) It's faster than thinking
- B) The structure carries your judgement when stress degrades it — you fall back on training, not rise to the occasion
- C) It removes the need to decide
- D) It guarantees the right answer

> **Best answer: B** — You don't rise to the occasion; you fall to your level of training. Structure is what you fall back on.

**Q3. In a plain-language structured decision loop, the step people most often skip is:**
- A) Detecting the decision point
- B) Evaluating whether the chosen action is actually working, and revising if not
- C) Acting
- D) Estimating urgency

> **Best answer: B** — "Evaluate / revise" is the antidote to defending the first choice — and it's the step most often dropped.

**Q4. "Pooling information before committing" is powerful because:**
- A) It spreads blame across the team
- B) The fact that would change the decision often sits with the quietest person and won't surface unless the leader asks
- C) It slows every decision down equally
- D) It lets the leader avoid responsibility

> **Best answer: B** — Asking "what am I missing?" pulls the decision-changing information out *before* the commit, not after the consequence.
