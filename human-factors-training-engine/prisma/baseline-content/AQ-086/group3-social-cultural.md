# Section 3 — The Social & Cultural Precursors

> **Section goal**
>
> Teach the final four — the ones that live between people and inside the group's habits: lack of teamwork, pressure, lack of assertiveness, and norms. The learner leaves able to define each, spot it, state the consequence, and name the safeguard — and to see why these four are the hardest to fix because they're about culture, not just individuals.

**LAYER B — TEACH THE SUBJECT**

### 7. The teach-out

The last group of precursors is the hardest, because you can't fix them alone — they live in how the *group* works and how the *organization* pushes. Two here: the team failing to back each other up, and the clock forcing shortcuts.

**Lack of teamwork.** *Define it:* people not coordinating, not backing each other up, not cross-checking — everyone heads-down on their own piece with no one watching the seams. *Why it bites:* maintenance is full of points where a second set of eyes catches what the first missed — the independent inspection, the cross-check on a critical task, the colleague who notices you skipped a step. When teamwork breaks down, those catches don't happen, and a single person's error has no second line of defense. Poor teamwork also means information doesn't flow and people don't speak up. *How it shows up at AeroMach:* a critical assembly that *should* get an independent cross-check gets signed by one person working in isolation because "we were short-handed and everyone was busy." *Consequence:* a single point of failure with no backup catch. *The safeguard:* **build in cross-checks and independent verification on critical work, and actively back each other up.** Define which tasks require a second person or an independent inspection — and protect that step even when busy. Encourage people to check on and help one another. A good team is a system of mutual error-catching; that's not overhead, it's a defense layer.

**Pressure.** *Define it:* the force — real or self-imposed — to get the job done faster than it can be done safely. Real pressure comes from schedules, an aircraft on the ground costing money, a customer waiting. Self-imposed pressure comes from inside: not wanting to be the one who's slow, the one who holds things up. *Why it bites:* pressure pushes people to cut corners, skip verification, and accept "good enough" on things that must be exactly right. And it compounds every other precursor — pressure makes the fatigued push harder, the distracted hurry back, the under-resourced improvise. *How it shows up at AeroMach:* a tech is told the aircraft must move tonight, so he's tempted to skip the cooling-off wait or the full functional check to save twenty minutes. *Consequence:* a safety-critical step traded for schedule. *The safeguard:* **recognize pressure for what it is and push back with the real risk — make the unsafe shortcut visible.** Communicate the genuine consequence ("if we skip this check, here's what we can't guarantee"). Refuse to let schedule override a safety-critical step, and make sure leadership has explicitly said safety beats schedule — so the person under the clock isn't standing alone. Managing self-imposed pressure means giving yourself permission to take the time the task actually needs.

> **STOP & THINK**
>
> Notice the link between these two and the next lesson's pair. Lack of teamwork removes your *backup*; pressure supplies the *push.* The safeguard for both runs through *speaking up* — a cross-check only works if someone will voice the discrepancy, and pushing back on pressure is, by definition, an assertive act. That's exactly why the next lesson tackles assertiveness and norms: these four social precursors reinforce each other, and the antidote to all of them is a culture where it's safe and expected to speak.

In AQ-083 terms, lack of teamwork removes a *defense layer* in the Swiss-Cheese model — it punches a hole in the very barrier (the independent check) that was supposed to catch the first error. Pressure is an organizational *latent condition*: a hole created up in planning and scheduling that lines up with the sharp-end slip. Name them, and a supervisor can defend the cross-check and surface the pressure before they line up.

### 8. Industry example

After a short-handed shift let a critical assembly go without its independent cross-check, AeroMach didn't blame the lone technician — Dana audited why the cross-check evaporated under load. The fix made critical-task cross-checks *non-negotiable*: if there's no second person available, the task waits, full stop, and that decision is backed by leadership in writing. On pressure, AeroMach made it explicit, top-down, that no schedule justifies skipping a safety-critical step — and gave technicians a script to make the risk visible to whoever's pushing. The cross-check and the "safety beats schedule" rule are designed safeguards, not appeals to heroism.

**LAYER B — TEACH THE SUBJECT**

### 7. The teach-out

These last two are the ones that separate a mature safety culture from a fragile one — because they're not about whether someone *knows* something is wrong, but about whether they'll *act* on it.

**Lack of assertiveness.** *Define it:* seeing a problem and not speaking up — or speaking up too weakly to be heard. The classic version is the *authority-gradient trap*: a junior person notices something wrong but doesn't challenge a senior, an experienced colleague, or a supervisor because of the rank, the experience gap, or the fear of looking foolish or being a nuisance. *Why it bites:* in a frightening number of failures, *someone knew.* Someone saw the discrepancy, felt the doubt — and stayed quiet, or raised it so softly it was brushed aside. The information that could have stopped the error existed in a human being who didn't voice it forcefully enough. *How it shows up at AeroMach:* a new tech, Priya's peer, notices the senior tech using what looks like the wrong sequence, but thinks "he's done this for twenty years, who am I to question him?" and says nothing. *Consequence:* a catchable error sails through because the catch was too polite to land. *The safeguard:* **build a speak-up / stop-the-job culture, and give people the words.** Assertiveness is a skill you can teach: state the concern clearly, name the specific risk, and persist until it's genuinely addressed — not aggressive, just clear and unwilling to be waved off. And the *other* half of the safeguard sits with seniority: the experienced person and the supervisor must visibly welcome the challenge, so speaking up is safe. Anyone, at any level, must be able to stop a job over a safety concern without penalty. A concern voiced and respected is a defense layer; a concern swallowed is a hole.

**Norms.** *Define it:* the unwritten "ways we do things around here" — the informal practices a group adopts that aren't in any procedure. *Why it's the sneakiest of all twelve:* a norm feels *safe precisely because everyone does it.* It carries the full weight of group approval, so it doesn't trip any alarm — and that's the trap. Bad norms form gradually: a shortcut works once, gets repeated, becomes "how we do it," and over time the actual procedure quietly drifts from the required one. Nobody decided to do it wrong; the group *drifted.* The most dangerous norm is the **normalization of a deviation** — a corner that's been cut so many times without consequence that it now feels like the correct method, and the person who follows the *real* procedure looks like the odd one out. *How it shows up at AeroMach:* "We always skip that intermediate check — it's never been a problem," said to a new hire as if it were wisdom. *Consequence:* a whole team confidently, collectively doing it the unsafe way, with social pressure on anyone who doesn't. *The safeguard:* **audit the norm against the requirement — surface unwritten practices and check them against the actual procedure.** Make it routine to ask "is the way we *actually* do this the way the procedure says?" and to investigate *why* a gap opened (often it reveals a procedure that's impractical and needs fixing — or a drift that needs stopping). Welcome the new person's "why do we do it this way?" as the most valuable question on the floor, not a naive one. And reinforce that "it's never been a problem yet" is not evidence of safety — it's the absence of consequence *so far*, which is exactly how a normalized deviation hides until the day it doesn't.

> **STOP & THINK**
>
> "It's never been a problem" is the single most dangerous sentence in maintenance, and it belongs to *norms.* It mistakes *luck* for *control.* Every normalized deviation has a perfect track record — right up until the conditions line up and it fails. When you hear it, you've found a norm to audit. And notice the link to assertiveness: it takes an *assertive* person to question a comfortable norm, and a *speak-up culture* to make that question welcome. These two precursors are joined at the hip — and they're why a healthy safety culture, not just trained individuals, is the ultimate safeguard.

This is where the Dirty Dozen meets **just culture** head-on, and it's the bridge to the final section. A bad norm or a swallowed concern is almost never an individual moral failure — it's a *system* that drifted or a culture that wasn't safe to speak in. If you respond to a normalized deviation by punishing the person who happened to be holding it when it failed, you teach everyone to hide the next one. Audit the process, not the person — and fix the conditions that let the norm form.

### 8. Industry example

Remember the "we always skip that intermediate check" line said to the new hire? At AeroMach, the new hire's "why is it in the procedure, then?" became a *good catch*, not an annoyance. Dana audited the norm against the requirement, found the intermediate check existed for a real failure mode, and — instead of disciplining a whole team that had drifted — fixed the conditions: re-trained on *why* the step exists, made norm-vs-procedure checks part of routine supervision, and publicly thanked the new hire for asking. On assertiveness, AeroMach gave everyone a stop-the-job right with no penalty and trained the senior techs to welcome a junior's challenge. The deviation didn't get someone fired; it got the system corrected. That's just culture and the norms safeguard working together.

- A) Norms
- B) Lack of assertiveness (the authority-gradient trap)
- C) Complacency
- D) Distraction

> **Best answer: B** — Seeing a problem and not speaking up — especially up a rank/experience gradient — is lack of assertiveness.

**Q2. The safeguard against lack of assertiveness is:**
- A) Tell juniors to stay quiet
- B) A speak-up / stop-the-job culture — teach people to state the concern and the risk clearly, and have seniors visibly welcome the challenge
- C) Only let supervisors raise concerns
- D) Discipline people who question others

> **Best answer: B** — A concern voiced and respected is a defense layer; the safeguard needs both the skill and a safe culture.

**Q3. Why are norms the sneakiest of the twelve?**
- A) They're written in the procedure
- B) A bad norm feels safe *because everyone does it* — group approval silences the alarm, and the deviation gets normalized until it fails
- C) They only affect new staff
- D) They're easy to spot

> **Best answer: B** — Normalization of deviation hides behind group consensus and a "never been a problem" track record.

**Q4. The safeguard against norms is:**
- A) Keep doing what everyone does
- B) Audit the norm against the requirement — surface unwritten practices, check them against the procedure, fix the gap, and treat "it's never been a problem" as luck, not proof
- C) Punish whoever's holding the deviation when it fails
- D) Speed up the unsafe practice

> **Best answer: B** — Check actual practice against the real procedure; "never been a problem yet" is absence of consequence, not control.
