# Section 1 — The Attention & Information Precursors

> **Section goal**
>
> Teach the first four of the twelve — the ones that corrupt the *information* you're working from or the *attention* you're working with: lack of communication, lack of knowledge, distraction, and lack of awareness. The learner leaves able to define each, spot it on the floor, state the consequence, and name the safeguard.

**LAYER B — TEACH THE SUBJECT**

### 7. The teach-out

The first two precursors both attack the same thing: the *information* you're working from. If the information is wrong, incomplete, or never arrived, even a perfect technician builds the wrong outcome perfectly. Let me take them in turn.

**Lack of communication.** *Define it:* a breakdown in the passing of information between people — a handover that didn't happen, an assumption that "someone told them," a verbal instruction nobody wrote down, a job left half-done with no note. *Why it bites:* most of the information that matters on a maintenance floor lives in people's heads and crosses between them at the riskiest moments — shift change, a job handed mid-task, a quick "can you finish this for me." Information passed by memory and assumption leaks. *How it shows up at AeroMach:* a night-shift tech leaves a flight-critical assembly with one fastener still to be torqued, tells nobody, and the day-shift tech sees a job that *looks* complete. *Consequence:* the un-torqued joint ships. *The safeguard:* **confirmed, documented handovers.** Don't pass critical status by memory — use the written handover, the job card, the task sign-off, and *confirm receipt* (the receiver reads it back). A handover isn't done when you've spoken; it's done when the other person has *understood*. Never leave a task in an ambiguous state — finish it or formally mark exactly where it stands.

**Lack of knowledge.** *Define it:* doing a task without the current data, the right training, or the correct understanding of how a system actually works. Note the trap — it's not stupidity. It's a competent person operating on out-of-date information or on a mental model that used to be right. *Why it bites:* technical data changes, parts get superseded, procedures get revised, and the most dangerous form of this is *not knowing what you don't know* — confidently doing it the old way. *How it shows up at AeroMach:* a skilled tech installs a component the way he's always done it, not realizing the manufacturer revised the torque sequence two revisions ago. *Consequence:* a defect built with full confidence — the hardest kind to catch, because nobody doubted it. *The safeguard:* **work from current, controlled data and ask when unsure.** Verify you have the latest revision before you start. Get the training before the task, not after the incident. And build a culture where "I'm not sure — let me check" is a sign of competence, not weakness. The technician who looks it up beats the one who's confidently wrong.

> **PAUSE & APPLY**
>
> Tell them apart, because the safeguard differs. **Lack of communication** = the information existed but didn't reach the person correctly → safeguard is *confirmed handovers and written records.* **Lack of knowledge** = the information was wrong, missing, or out of date in the person's head → safeguard is *current data and asking.* At AeroMach, a missed handover is a communication failure; an obsolete torque sequence is a knowledge failure. Different precursor, different defense.

Connect this back to the broader models from AQ-083: lack of communication is one of the classic *latent conditions* in the Swiss-Cheese model — a hole that sits open across a shift change. Lack of knowledge is a performance-shaping factor that degrades the human at the sharp end. Naming them as Dirty-Dozen precursors just makes them *operationally huntable* — something you can spot at the handover board or the data terminal.

### 8. Industry example

After the un-torqued-joint near-miss, Dana didn't write up the technician. She audited the *process*: the handover was verbal, mid-task, at shift change, with no written record of in-progress work. AeroMach's fix was a controlled handover log — every in-progress flight-critical task gets a written status and a read-back at shift change — plus a rule that no critical task is left in an ambiguous state. Separately, the obsolete-torque finding led to a data-currency check at the start of every job: confirm the revision before the first turn of the wrench. Two precursors, two safeguards, zero blame. That's the model.

- B) Lack of communication

> **Best answer: B** — Critical status passed (or not passed) by memory at a handover is the classic communication failure.

- B) Confirmed, documented handovers with read-back — never leave a critical task in an ambiguous state
- C) Trust that the next shift will notice

- A) Lack of knowledge
- B) Lack of resources
- C) Distraction

- B) Work from current controlled data, get training before the task, and ask when unsure

**LAYER B — TEACH THE SUBJECT**

### 7. The teach-out

The next two precursors both attack your *attention* — but in different ways. One is a sudden break in focus; the other is a slow loss of the big picture. Both are dangerous, and both have specific safeguards.

**Distraction.** *Define it:* anything that pulls your attention away mid-task — a phone call, a colleague's question, a radio, an alarm, a "quick favour." Here's the part people underestimate: the danger isn't the interruption itself, it's the *return.* When you come back to a task after a break, your brain has a powerful tendency to assume you did the step you were actually about to do. You skip forward. *Why it bites:* the human mind doesn't bookmark cleanly. After an interruption you genuinely *feel* like you completed the step you'd just reached, when in fact you were interrupted right before it. *How it shows up at AeroMach:* a tech is torquing a series of fasteners, gets called away to answer a question, comes back, and resumes one fastener *past* where he stopped — leaving one un-torqued, feeling certain he did them all. *Consequence:* a missed step inside a task that looks finished. *The safeguard:* **when interrupted, mark your place and re-verify on return.** Physically mark where you stopped (a tool left in place, a note, a checklist tick). When you come back, don't trust your memory — go back one step and re-confirm. Better still, finish the critical sub-task before you allow the interruption at all.

**Lack of awareness.** *Define it:* losing sight of the bigger picture — not seeing how your action affects the whole system, the next person, or the aircraft. It's the failure to maintain *situational awareness*: where you are in the job, what's around you, what your change will affect downstream. *Why it bites:* it's possible to do your individual step perfectly and still cause harm because you didn't see the consequence two steps over — you didn't notice the part you moved blocks someone else's access, or the system you isolated feeds something still in use. *How it shows up at AeroMach:* a tech completes his task correctly but doesn't register that an adjacent panel is still open and a colleague is working live beneath where he's about to set a heavy fixture. *Consequence:* a hazard created by tunnel-vision on one's own task. *The safeguard:* **deliberately keep the big picture — pause and scan.** Before and after a task, look up: what's around me, who else is affected, what does my change touch downstream? Brief and de-brief the job in the context of the whole. Situational awareness isn't automatic under load — you have to *choose* to lift your eyes.

> **STOP & THINK**
>
> Distraction and lack of awareness feel similar but they're opposites in time. **Distraction** is a *sudden* loss of focus on the task in front of you → safeguard is *mark and re-verify after the interruption.* **Lack of awareness** is a *gradual* loss of the picture *around* the task → safeguard is *deliberately scan the big picture.* One is "I lost my place in the step." The other is "I never saw the step next to me." Both kill — and the most dangerous interruptions are the ones you welcome because they feel social and harmless.

Tie it to AQ-083: distraction and lack of awareness are textbook *performance-shaping factors* that degrade attention and situational awareness — and they're exactly the human limits the SHELL model says you have to design the *environment* around, not just exhort the person to overcome. The Dirty Dozen makes them concrete enough to defend against at the bench.

### 8. Industry example

Sam, the assembly-cell supervisor, started a simple rule after a distraction near-miss: on any flight-critical torque or lockwire sequence, if you're interrupted you *go back one fastener and re-check* before continuing — no exceptions, and the rule protects the person, not blames them. He also added a thirty-second "look up and around" at the start and end of each job — a deliberate situational-awareness scan. Neither rule depends on anyone being more careful by willpower. They're designed safeguards against two precursors that no amount of "pay attention" ever fixed. Audit the process: give people a defense, don't just demand vigilance.

### 15. Knowledge Check — graded • 4 questions • 80% to pass

**Q1. The most dangerous moment in a distraction is:**
- A) The interruption itself
- B) The *return* — the mind assumes the step you were about to do is done, and you skip forward
- C) Before the task starts
- D) After the shift ends

> **Best answer: B** — The brain doesn't bookmark cleanly; on return you feel certain you completed a step you actually missed.

**Q2. The safeguard against distraction is:**
- A) Never take a break
- B) Mark your place when interrupted and re-verify (go back a step) on return — or finish the critical sub-task before allowing the interruption
- C) Work faster after the interruption
- D) Trust your memory of where you were

> **Best answer: B** — Mark and re-verify; don't trust memory across an interruption.

**Q3. A technician does his own task perfectly but doesn't notice a colleague working live in the area his change affects. The precursor is:**
- A) Distraction
- B) Lack of awareness (loss of the big picture / situational awareness)
- C) Fatigue
- D) Lack of resources

> **Best answer: B** — Doing your step right but missing its effect on the system/people around you is a situational-awareness failure.

**Q4. The safeguard against lack of awareness is:**
- A) Focus only on your own task and ignore everything else
- B) Deliberately keep the big picture — pause and scan what's around you and what your change affects downstream
- C) Work with headphones in
- D) Assume someone else is watching the system

> **Best answer: B** — Situational awareness isn't automatic under load; you have to choose to lift your eyes and scan.
