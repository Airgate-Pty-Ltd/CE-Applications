### Learning objectives

*After this lesson the learner can:*
- Run an interview that gets honest, accurate recollection — open, one-at-a-time, soon after, non-blaming.
- Recognize and avoid leading questions and the blame framing that distorts answers.
- Explain why poor interview technique corrupts the evidence the whole analysis rests on.

### The teach-out

The interview is where most of your *human* evidence comes from — and it's a genuine craft, because a badly-run interview doesn't just fail to get the truth, it actively *manufactures* false evidence that feels solid. Let me teach you to run one well, because the analysis is only as good as the facts you feed it, and a lot of those facts come from this room.

Start with the four conditions of a good interview, each with a *why*:

- **Open, not leading.** *Define a leading question:* one that contains the answer you expect, so the witness just agrees. "You forgot to check the torque, didn't you?" *plants* a fact; the witness, especially a nervous one, says "yeah, I guess I did," and you've recorded as evidence something *you* supplied, not something they actually remember. An **open** question asks them to tell you, without steering: "Walk me through what you did, step by step." "What happened next?" "Tell me about that part of the shift." Open questions retrieve *their* memory; leading questions overwrite it with yours. This is the single biggest interview error, and it's invisible — the false fact looks just like a real one in your notes.
- **One person at a time.** Interview people *separately*. The moment you talk to a group, accounts converge — the confident voice in the room becomes everyone's memory, and the quiet person who actually saw the key detail defers and stays quiet. Separate interviews preserve the *independent* recollections, which is where the detail that cracks the case usually lives.
- **Soon after.** Memory decays fast; interview while it's fresh, before detail fades and before people have rehearsed a converged story. (This is why Section 2 captured names and initial accounts in the first hour.)
- **Non-blaming.** The framing you walk in with determines what you walk out with. If the witness believes the interview is hunting for who's at fault, they self-protect — they give you the minimum, the safe version, and the truth that might implicate them or a colleague stays buried. The non-blaming frame — *"I'm trying to understand what made this easy to get wrong, not who to blame"* — is what unlocks the honest account. This is **just culture** applied at the interview table, and it's not softness: it's the only way to keep the evidence honest.

> **STOP & THINK**
>
> Here's the move that separates an interviewer from an interrogator: **your job is to retrieve the witness's memory, not to confirm your theory.** The instant you start asking questions that test *your* hunch — "so the procedure was clear, right?" "you were rushing, weren't you?" — you've stopped investigating and started *fishing for agreement*, and a nervous, eager-to-please, or self-protecting witness will hand you the agreement you're fishing for. Now your "evidence" is just your own assumption echoed back in someone else's voice — and it's *indistinguishable* in your notes from a real recollection. So discipline yourself to open questions ("tell me what happened," "what did you do next," "walk me through it") and to genuine curiosity about *their* account, not yours. And keep the frame on the *process*: "audit the process, not the person" lives or dies right here — ask what made the error *easy*, and people tell you the truth; ask whose fault it was, and they tell you nothing. The interview that gets the truth is the one where the witness isn't afraid of you and isn't being steered by you.

So a good interview: separate, soon, open, and safe. You let them tell the story in their own words first (open, uninterrupted), *then* go back and clarify specifics — still without leading. You're not there to corner anyone or to confirm what you already think; you're there to retrieve, as cleanly as possible, what they actually saw and did. Get this wrong — lead them, blame them, interview them in a group a week later — and you've fed your timeline and your analysis a set of facts that *feel* solid and are quietly false. Get it right, and you get the one honest sentence that often *is* the investigation.

### Industry example

AeroMach's fastener event, the interview run two ways. **The wrong way** (the old AeroMach): Dana's predecessor sits the technician down with the supervisor present and opens, "You used the wrong torque value — were you not paying attention?" The technician, cornered and watched, says "I guess I wasn't, sorry, won't happen again." Recorded as evidence: "technician inattentive." It's a *manufactured* fact — the investigator supplied "inattentive," the cornered technician agreed, and the real cause is now buried under a false confession. **The right way** (Dana): she interviews him alone, soon after, and opens, "I'm not looking for who to blame — I'm trying to understand what made this easy to get wrong. Walk me through your shift, step by step." Uninterrupted, he tells it: two near-identical assemblies on adjacent benches, an interruption mid-job, a return, and reaching for the value he uses most shifts because the traveller didn't put it in front of him at the install step. *That* account — honest, specific, unled — points straight at the look-alike parts, the interruptible procedure, and the buried value: the real causes. Priya's note: "Same technician, same event. The leading, blaming interview *made up* a cause — 'inattentive' — and hid the real one. The open, safe interview got the truth in two minutes. The interview wasn't admin. It *was* the evidence."

### Reflection

Write one **leading** question you've heard (or might ask) about your event — then rewrite it as an **open** one. (E.g. "You skipped the check, didn't you?" → "Walk me through the checks you did.") Now write the one-sentence **non-blaming frame** you'd open the interview with. Would the people involved tell you the truth — or the safe version?

### Knowledge Check — graded • 4 questions • 80% to pass

**Q1. A leading question is dangerous because it:**
- A) Takes too long
- B) Plants the answer you expect — the witness agrees, and you record as evidence something *you* supplied, not what they actually remember
- C) Is too open
- D) Confuses the timeline

> **Best answer: B** — Leading questions overwrite the witness's memory with yours; the false fact looks real in your notes.

**Q2. Witnesses should be interviewed one at a time because:**
- A) It's faster
- B) Group interviews converge accounts — the confident voice becomes everyone's memory and the quiet person who saw the key detail defers — so you lose independent recollection
- C) It's more comfortable
- D) Regulations require it

> **Best answer: B** — Separate interviews preserve the independent detail that often cracks the case.

**Q3. The non-blaming frame ("I'm trying to understand what made this easy to get wrong, not who to blame") matters because:**
- A) It's polite
- B) The frame determines the answer — a witness who fears blame gives the safe minimum; a witness who feels safe gives the honest account
- C) It speeds the interview
- D) It's required wording

> **Best answer: B** — Just culture at the interview table is what keeps the human evidence honest.

**Q4. The interviewer's core job is to:**
- A) Confirm the theory they walked in with
- B) Retrieve the witness's actual memory as cleanly as possible — open questions, genuine curiosity about their account, not fishing for agreement
- C) Get a confession
- D) Decide on discipline

> **Best answer: B** — Fishing for agreement turns your assumption into "evidence" in someone else's voice.
