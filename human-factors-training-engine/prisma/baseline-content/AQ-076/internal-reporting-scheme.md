### Learning objectives

*After this lesson the learner can:*
- List the elements of an actionable hazard report (what, where/when, who/what it could harm, risk read, immediate action, category).
- Explain why each element exists — what the system can't do without it.
- Recognize and avoid the four report failure modes: vague, blameful, buried, late.

### The teach-out

Here's the skill that separates a participant from someone who's merely aware: a participant writes a report the system can *act on without coming back to ask questions.* Let me define the standard, then explain why every piece of it earns its place.

An **actionable hazard report** has six parts. **What you observed** — the condition or object, factually. **Where and when** — so someone can find it and fix it. **Who or what it could harm** — product, people, or operation/customer. **Your risk read** — how likely and how severe, even roughly. **Any immediate action you took** — did you quarantine the part, tag the tool, wipe the spill, or is it untouched and you're escalating? And **the category** — is this a hazard (could cause harm), a near-miss (almost did), or an unsafe condition (it's wrong right now)?

Now *why* does each piece matter — what does the system lose without it? Drop the **where/when** and the hazard can't be located; Tom gets "a tool is overdue" and has no idea which tool at which station. Drop the **risk read** and the hazard can't be *prioritized* — it sits in a queue with everything else, and the catastrophic one waits behind a paper cut. Drop the **immediate action** and the next person doesn't know whether the bad tool is still in service right now. Drop the **category** and the system can't route it. Every missing piece is a phone call Tom has to make back to you, or — far worse — a hazard that stalls because nobody chased the missing fact. **A complete report is a hazard the system can act on at 2 a.m. without you in the room.** That's the bar.

> **STOP & THINK**
>
> The single most important word in that whole report is in "what you observed": describe the **condition, not a person.** "The torque wrench at station 4 is two weeks past calibration and being used on flight-critical assemblies" — that's a condition the system can fix. "Marcus keeps using bad tools" — that's an accusation the system can't fix and that just taught everyone watching that reporting gets a coworker in trouble. Audit the process, not the person, starts in the *first sentence of your report.* Get that wrong and you don't just file a weak report — you poison the reporting culture for everyone who sees how it landed.

Let me name the four ways a report gets ignored, because avoiding them *is* the skill. **Vague:** "something's unsafe in receiving" — un-actionable, can't be located or sized. **Blameful:** names or blames a person — un-actionable and culture-damaging. **Buried:** mentioned in passing in a hallway or buried in a shift note where the system never sees it — there's no record, so there's no action. **Late:** filed two weeks after you saw it, by which point the trigger may already have arrived. A good participant kills all four: specific, about the condition, in the actual channel, promptly. None of that requires a safety degree. It requires the decision to do it properly.

And *what happens if you get it wrong* — if you file the vague, late, blameful version? Best case, Tom chases you for the facts and the fix is delayed. Worst case, the report is so weak it gets closed as "insufficient information," the hazard stays live, and the next person who sees a similar issue notices that reporting it accomplished nothing — so they stop. A bad report doesn't just fail to fix one hazard; it teaches the floor that reporting doesn't work.

### Industry example

Two reports about the same hazard landed on Tom's desk the same week. The first: "Receiving is a mess, someone's going to get hurt, Priya needs to sort it out." Tom couldn't act on it — no location, no specific condition, and it blamed a person. It got closed as insufficient information, and the hazard stayed. The second, from Sofia, who'd been coached: "Heavy crate (~40 kg) stored on the top shelf of rack R3 in receiving, no handling aid available; could fall during retrieval and injure whoever's reaching for it. Likelihood moderate, severity serious (back/crush injury). I've put a 'do not retrieve unaided' note on it pending a fix. Category: unsafe condition." Tom actioned it that afternoon — ordered a step platform, moved the crate down, closed the loop with Sofia. Same hazard. One report changed nothing and quietly damaged the culture; the other fixed a real risk and *built* the culture, because Sofia got a visible result. That delta is this entire lesson.

### Reflection

Take a real hazard you've seen (yours or AeroMach's). Write the *one-sentence "what I observed"* line two ways: once vaguely or with blame, once as a specific, person-free condition. Feel the difference. The second one is the only one the system can act on.

### Knowledge Check — graded • 4 questions • 80% to pass

**Q1. An actionable hazard report includes, at minimum:**
- A) Just a description of the problem
- B) What you observed, where/when, who/what it could harm, a risk read, any immediate action, and the category
- C) The name of who caused it
- D) A proposed disciplinary action

> **Best answer: B** — Each element is something the system needs to locate, prioritize, route, and act without coming back to you.

**Q2. The "what I observed" line should describe:**
- A) The person responsible
- B) The condition or object, factually — not a person
- C) Your opinion of who's to blame
- D) Nothing specific, to avoid singling anyone out

> **Best answer: B** — Audit the process, not the person, starts in the first sentence. A condition can be fixed; an accusation can't, and it damages the culture.

**Q3. Leaving the risk read out of a report mainly hurts because:**
- A) It makes the report shorter
- B) The hazard can't be *prioritized* — it sits in the queue and the catastrophic one waits behind trivial ones
- C) Risk reads are not allowed
- D) Only managers can judge risk

> **Best answer: B** — Without a rough likelihood-and-severity read, the system can't triage. Your read is what moves the serious one to the front.

**Q4. The four ways a report gets ignored are:**
- A) Too long, too formal, too detailed, too honest
- B) Vague, blameful, buried (not in the channel), and late
- C) Typed, handwritten, verbal, emailed
- D) Anonymous, signed, urgent, routine

> **Best answer: B** — Specific, about the condition, in the real channel, promptly — kill those four failure modes and your report gets actioned.
