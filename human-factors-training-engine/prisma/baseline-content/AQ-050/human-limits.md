# Section 1 — Why Good People Make Errors: Human Limits

> **Section goal**
>
> Make the learner fluent in the single most useful idea in the course: error is normal, and it comes from human limits that are predictable. Then connect those limits — attention, memory, fatigue, stress, workload — to the everyday quality failures they already recognize, so the "designing out" in Section 3 feels earned.

## Lesson 1.1 — Error Is Normal: The Limits Everyone Shares

### Learning objectives
*After this lesson the learner can:*
- Explain why competent, careful people still make errors (the limits are universal).
- Name the core human limits that drive quality error: narrow attention, fragile working memory.
- Reframe "I was sure I did that" and the missed step as predictable, not careless.

### Competency & Bloom
| Competency | Learning outcome | Bloom's level |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Human limits | Explain error as a predictable output of normal human limits | Understand |

### The teach-out

Here's the foundation everything else rests on, and it's worth saying without flinching: **error is normal.** Not a sign of a bad employee. Not a discipline problem. A normal output of a normal human nervous system doing a demanding task. The best operator on AeroMach's floor — twenty years of experience, takes pride in the work, has never been written up — will still make errors, because the limits that produce error are *human*, not personal. You cannot hire your way out of them, and you cannot train your way out of them. You design around them.

Let me name the two limits that drive most quality error, in plain language first.

**The first is attention — and the key fact is that attention is narrow.** You can only consciously focus on a small slice of what's in front of you at once. The brain handles this by putting routine tasks on a kind of autopilot, which is wonderful — it's why an experienced operator is fast — but autopilot has a failure mode: when something interrupts you, or a step is slightly different from the usual, attention is somewhere else, and the autopilot runs the *usual* pattern instead of the *correct* one. That's not a lapse in caring. That's how attention works.

**The second is working memory — and the key fact is that working memory is small and fragile.** The mental "scratchpad" where you hold what you're doing *right now* — which step you're on, what you were about to grab, the count you're keeping — holds only a few items, and it gets wiped by interruption almost instantly. The classic quality failure "I was *sure* I'd already torqued that one" is not a lie and usually not even a mistake of skill. It's working memory being interrupted between *intending* the step and *executing* it, leaving a confident, false memory that the step was done.

> **STOP & THINK**
>
> Notice what we have *not* said. We haven't said the operator was lazy, distracted in a blameworthy way, or undertrained. We've said: attention is narrow, memory is fragile, and both were operating exactly as human attention and memory always operate. If the error came from a *normal* limit, then the fix can't be "be abnormal" — "try harder to remember," "concentrate more." The fix has to come from the *work.* That's the whole pivot of this course, and you just made it.

So why does this matter for a quality system? Because it tells you where defects actually come from, and therefore where to put your effort. If you believe defects come from people not caring, you invest in posters, pep talks, and discipline — and the defects keep coming, because you never touched the cause. If you believe defects come from normal limits meeting demanding work, you invest in *work design* — and the defects drop. Same defect, two completely different responses. This course is going to make you the second kind of quality professional.

### Industry example

AeroMach's most experienced assembler missed a step on a routine build — left out a small spacer. Sam, the supervisor, was ready to write "experienced operator got complacent." Dana asked what happened. The assembler had been interrupted mid-build by a question from a new hire, answered it, and came back *certain* he'd already placed the spacer — because in his memory, he had. Twenty years of skill didn't protect him; in a way it *exposed* him, because the autopilot that makes an expert fast is the same autopilot that resumes the wrong step after an interruption. **The error wasn't a gap in his competence. It was a gap in the work — there was no interruption-resistant way to know which step he was actually on.** Hold that; in Section 3 we close exactly that gap.

### Reflection
> Recall a time you were sure you'd done something — locked a door, sent a file, completed a step — and you hadn't. What interrupted you between intending it and doing it? That's working memory, not character.

- B) Error is a normal output of normal human limits — even competent, careful people make errors, so we design work to prevent and catch it
- C) Only new employees make errors
- D) Errors only happen when people don't care

> **Best answer: B** — Error comes from universal human limits, not character. That's why the response is design, not blame.

**Q2. The quality failure "I was sure I'd already done that step" is best explained by:**
- A) Dishonesty
- B) Fragile working memory wiped by an interruption between intending and executing the step
- C) Lack of training
- D) A bad attitude

> **Best answer: B** — Working memory is small and interruption-sensitive; it leaves a confident false memory. A normal limit, not a character flaw.

**Q3. If defects come mainly from normal human limits, the most effective place to invest is:**
- A) Motivational posters and pep talks
- B) Discipline for the people involved
- C) The design of the work itself, so the error is harder to make
- D) Faster hiring

> **Best answer: C** — You can't train people out of being human; you design the work to fit the human you have.

## Lesson 1.2 — Fatigue, Stress, Workload: The Dials That Turn Up Error

### Learning objectives
*After this lesson the learner can:*
- Explain how fatigue, stress, and high workload degrade attention and memory predictably.
- Recognize the end-of-shift, overtime, and "rush job" windows as elevated-error conditions.
- Frame these as conditions to manage, not personal weaknesses to scold.

### Competency & Bloom
| Competency | Learning outcome | Bloom's level |
| --- | --- | --- |
| State factors | Explain how fatigue/stress/workload amplify error risk | Understand / Apply |

### The teach-out

In Lesson 1.1 we said attention and memory are limited. Now the bad news: those limits aren't fixed — they get *worse*, predictably, under certain conditions. Think of them as dials. Three dials turn error risk up, and a quality professional who knows the dials knows exactly when a process is most likely to bite.

**Dial one — fatigue.** A tired brain is, measurably, a worse brain at exactly the things quality depends on: sustained attention, accurate memory, catching your own mistakes. The dangerous part is that fatigue degrades your *self-monitoring* — the tired person is the last to know how impaired they are. This is why the **end of a long shift, the overtime hours, the back-to-back rotation** are not just tiring; they are statistically *error-rich windows.* When you find that a defect cluster happens late on the shift or during a crunch of overtime, you haven't found a discipline problem. You've found fatigue doing exactly what fatigue does.

**Dial two — stress and time pressure.** Under pressure, attention *narrows* — useful in a real emergency, dangerous on a precision task, because the narrowed focus drops the very cross-checks that catch error. "We have to ship this today" doesn't make people careless; it makes their attention tunnel onto *getting it out* and away from *getting it right.* Time pressure is probably the single most common error-amplifier on a shop floor, and we'll meet it again as a named performance-shaping factor in Section 2.

**Dial three — workload, in both directions.** Too much at once — juggling three jobs, constant interruptions — overloads the narrow attention and the small working memory, and steps get dropped. But here's the part people miss: **too little also raises error.** A monotonous, under-stimulating task lets attention drift; the mind wanders, and the routine check gets a routine glance instead of a real look. High load drops steps; low load drops vigilance. Both are real, and both are about the task, not the person.

> **PAUSE & APPLY**
>
> AeroMach is running mandatory overtime to recover a late shipment. Knowing the three dials, *predict* where the next defect is most likely: late in the shift (fatigue), on the rushed jobs (time pressure), and on whichever operators are juggling the most at once (overload). You can almost draw a target on the schedule. That predictability is the gift — if you can predict the error-rich window, you can put extra defenses there *before* the defect, instead of writing it up after.

So what do you *do* with the dials? You stop treating fatigue, pressure, and overload as excuses and start treating them as **conditions to manage**: realistic schedules and rotation on critical work, not stacking the hardest tasks at the end of a double; protecting time on safety- or quality-critical steps instead of rushing them; balancing workload so nobody is juggling four jobs through one bottleneck. None of that is soft. It's defect prevention through the one lever that actually moves the human limits: the conditions you put people in.

### Industry example

AeroMach's escape — a mis-machined feature that reached the customer — landed on the last lot of a Friday double shift, run on overtime to clear a backlog. Sam's first instinct: "the operator rushed and got sloppy." Dana mapped the rejects against the schedule and the pattern was undeniable — the defects clustered in the final two hours of extended shifts, across *different* operators. **That's not three careless people. That's fatigue and end-of-shift time pressure, turned up by overtime, doing exactly what the dials predict.** The fix wasn't to scold three good operators; it was to stop scheduling the most demanding, lowest-tolerance work into the most error-rich window. The dial was the cause. The operators were just standing where the dial pointed.

### Reflection
> In your own work, when is the error-rich window? End of shift? During the monthly close? During a rush order? Name it. If you can name it, you can defend it.

### Knowledge Check — graded • 4 questions • 80% to pass

**Q1. Fatigue is especially dangerous to quality because:**
- A) Tired people are lazy
- B) It degrades sustained attention and memory AND degrades self-monitoring — the tired person is the last to notice they're impaired
- C) It only affects new employees
- D) It has no measurable effect

> **Best answer: B** — Fatigue hits the very abilities quality depends on, and hides itself from the person experiencing it.

**Q2. Time pressure increases error mainly by:**
- A) Making people not care
- B) Narrowing attention onto "getting it out," which drops the cross-checks that catch error
- C) Improving focus on quality
- D) Having no effect on skilled workers

> **Best answer: B** — Pressure tunnels attention toward speed and away from the checks that catch mistakes.

**Q3. Regarding workload and error:**
- A) Only high workload raises error
- B) Both too-high (overload drops steps) and too-low (monotony drops vigilance) raise error
- C) Workload doesn't affect error
- D) Low workload always means high quality

> **Best answer: B** — High load drops steps; low load drops vigilance. Both are task conditions, not character.

**Q4. A defect cluster in the last two hours of extended overtime shifts, across different operators, most likely indicates:**
- A) Three careless operators who need discipline
- B) An error-rich window (fatigue + end-of-shift pressure) — a condition to manage, not three people to blame
- C) A coincidence to ignore
- D) A training gap

> **Best answer: B** — Same window, different people = the condition, not the person. Audit the process.
