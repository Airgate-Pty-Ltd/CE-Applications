# Section 2 — Hazard Identification: Find Them on Purpose

> **Section goal**
>
> Make the learner fluent in finding hazards from all three source types (reactive, proactive, predictive) and — the make-or-break skill — telling a *hazard* apart from its *consequence*. They leave able to write a clean hazard statement and explain why reactive-only hazard ID keeps an organisation permanently surprised.

## Lesson 2.1 — Reactive, Proactive, Predictive: Three Ways to Find Hazards

### Learning Objectives
*After this lesson the learner can:*
- Define a hazard and distinguish the three source types: reactive, proactive, predictive.
- Explain why a system that finds hazards only reactively stays permanently surprised.
- List concrete hazard sources for each type.

### Competency & Bloom
| Competency | Learning outcome | Bloom's level |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Hazard identification | Find hazards from reactive, proactive and predictive sources | Understand / Apply |

### The Teach-Out

Define it before we use it: a **hazard** is a *condition, object, or situation with the potential to cause harm* — injury, damage, or loss. Notice the word *potential*. A hazard doesn't have to have hurt anyone yet. That's the whole point of finding them — you want them *before* the harm, not in the accident report. An open pinch point is a hazard whether or not anyone's been caught in it. Fuel vapour near an ignition source is a hazard on a day nothing happens.

Now the skill: hazards come from three kinds of sources, and a healthy SRM process feeds on all three.

**Reactive sources** look *backward* at things that already went wrong. Accident and incident reports, injuries, near-misses, returned product, regulatory findings. Reactive sources are valuable — they're real, they're proven, nobody argues with them. But here's the catch I want you to never forget: **if reactive is your *only* source, every hazard you find has already hurt something.** You're paying for your hazard list in damage. Reactive-only hazard ID is how organisations stay permanently surprised — they only learn a hazard existed on the day it bites.

**Proactive sources** look at the system *now*, before it fails. Hazard reports from the floor, safety surveys, audits, inspections, job/task analysis, trend data, and the simplest, most underrated one — *walking the floor and asking the people who do the work.* Proactive sources cost you attention instead of damage. This is where a mature SMS lives: finding hazards while they're still just conditions, not yet events.

**Predictive sources** look *forward* and ask "what could go wrong that hasn't yet?" — analysing data and changes to anticipate hazards in conditions that don't exist yet. Modelling a new process before it runs, analysing flight or operational data for emerging patterns, studying a planned change for the hazards it'll introduce. Predictive is the hardest and the most powerful: it can catch a hazard before the system that contains it even exists.

> **PAUSE & APPLY**
>
> The three types as a timeline. **Reactive** = the hazard already caused harm (you learn from the wreckage). **Proactive** = the hazard exists now but hasn't caused harm yet (you find it by looking). **Predictive** = the hazard doesn't exist yet but will if a change goes through (you anticipate it). A serious SRM process uses all three — but the *health* of your system is measured by how much of your hazard flow comes from proactive and predictive sources instead of reactive ones. Lots of reactive findings and few proactive ones means you're learning from damage, not from looking.

*How do you prove your hazard ID is real?* A *steady flow* of identified hazards from multiple sources — including near-misses and hazard reports, not just injuries. A reporting culture where people actually file. Evidence that audits, trends and changes feed the process. *What happens if it fails?* Your hazard log goes quiet — not because there are no hazards, but because nobody's looking or nobody feels safe reporting — and the next thing in the log is an accident.

### Industry Example

AeroMach's pinch point shows up reactively — an operator reports it Monday after nearly getting caught. Good: the report happened, the culture worked. But Marcus asks the harder question: *why did we only find this reactively?* The answer is that the weekend process change skipped the predictive step — nobody analysed the new fixture *before* it ran, which is exactly when this hazard was both findable and cheapest to fix. And proactively, no one had walked the new cell during its first shift. So AeroMach found a real hazard the expensive way. Marcus's takeaway isn't "good catch" — it's "our reactive net worked, but our proactive and predictive nets weren't even cast." That's the gap. A process change should have been caught looking *forward*, not reported looking *back*.

### Reflection
> Of the last three hazards your organisation found, how many came reactively (after harm) versus proactively or predictively? What does that ratio tell you?

- A) An injury that has occurred
- B) A condition, object or situation with the *potential* to cause harm
- C) The same as a risk score
- D) Only something that has already caused damage

> **Best answer: B** — The defining word is *potential* — a hazard exists before the harm.

**Q2. Reactive hazard sources:**
- A) Anticipate hazards before they exist
- B) Look backward at events that already caused harm (accidents, incidents, near-misses, findings)
- C) Are the healthiest sign of a mature SMS
- D) Should be the only source used

> **Best answer: B** — Reactive looks back at proven harm; valuable but, if used alone, you only learn from damage.

**Q3. Predictive hazard identification:**
- A) Waits for an incident report
- B) Looks forward to anticipate hazards in conditions or changes that don't exist yet
- C) Is the same as proactive
- D) Is illegal in aviation

> **Best answer: B** — Predictive anticipates future hazards — e.g. analysing a planned change before it runs.

**Q4. An organisation that finds hazards *only* reactively will:**
- A) Have the safest possible system
- B) Stay permanently surprised — every hazard it finds has already caused harm
- C) Never need controls
- D) Pass every audit

> **Best answer: B** — Reactive-only means you pay for every hazard in damage. Mature systems weight toward proactive/predictive.

## Lesson 2.2 — Hazard vs Consequence: The Distinction That Saves the Whole Process

### Learning Objectives
*After this lesson the learner can:*
- Distinguish a hazard from its consequence and from a contributing condition.
- Write a clean hazard statement that names the hazard, not the outcome.
- Explain why confusing the two breaks risk analysis and control selection.

### Competency & Bloom
| Competency | Learning outcome | Bloom's level |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Hazard articulation | Separate hazard, consequence and condition; write a clean hazard statement | Apply |

### The Teach-Out

This is the single most common mistake in all of SRM, and getting it right is worth more than any matrix: **people confuse the hazard with its consequence.** Let me define the three things you have to keep straight.

- A **hazard** is the *condition with potential to cause harm* — the unguarded pinch point, the fuel vapour, the fatigued worker, the uncalibrated torque wrench.
- A **consequence** is the *harm that results if the hazard is realised* — the crushed hand, the fire, the missed defect, the over-torqued fastener that fails in service.
- A **contributing condition** (or contributing factor) is something that makes the hazard *more likely to be realised or worse* — poor lighting, time pressure, a missing guard, lack of training.

Here's why this matters so much, and it's not academic. **If you name the consequence as the hazard, you'll try to control the consequence — which is impossible.** "Crushed hand" is not a hazard you can guard, train, or eliminate. You can only control the *hazard that causes it*: the unguarded pinch point. Get the hazard statement wrong and every step that follows — analysis, control selection, residual risk — is aimed at the wrong target. The whole loop misfires.

A clean way to write a hazard statement is to name the **hazard**, then trace it to the **consequence** through the **conditions**: *"[Hazard] could lead to [consequence], made more likely/worse by [conditions]."* For example: *"An unguarded pinch point at the clamping station (hazard) could crush an operator's hand (consequence), made more likely by the re-seat-by-hand step and made worse by two operators new to the fixture (conditions)."* Now you can see exactly what to control — the pinch point and the conditions — and exactly what you're trying to prevent — the crush.

> **STOP & THINK**
>
> Quick test you can use forever: ask *"can I put a guard, a procedure, or a design change on this?"* If yes, it's probably the hazard. If the only honest answer is "you can't guard a broken arm," you've named the consequence — back up and find the condition that causes it. You control hazards and conditions. You *prevent* consequences. Never the other way round.

One more trap, because it's the auditor's favourite: a hazard statement that's so vague it can't be analysed. "Safety culture" is not a hazard. "Human error" is not a hazard. They're too broad to score or control. Push to the specific condition: not "human error" but "an operator can reach into an unguarded pinch point while clearing a misseat." Specific enough to score, specific enough to control. *How do you prove you've done this well?* Hazard statements in your register that name real conditions, traceable to consequences. *What happens if you get it wrong?* You'll spend the rest of the process controlling outcomes you can't touch, and your "controls" will be wishes.

### Industry Example

When Priya's operator first reports the problem, the words are *"someone's going to lose a hand on that machine"* — that's the **consequence**, and it's a perfectly good alarm. But Marcus's job is to translate it into something the process can actually work on. He writes the hazard, not the fear: *"Unguarded pinch point at the actuator-housing clamping station (hazard) could crush an operator's hand (consequence); more likely because the fault-clearing step requires reaching in by hand, and worse because both operators are new to this fixture (conditions)."* Now the team can see precisely what to attack: guard the pinch point, design out the reach-in step, train the operators. If Marcus had logged "crushed hand" as the hazard, the team would have stared at it with nothing to do — you can't control a crushed hand. Naming the hazard correctly is what turns a fear into a fixable problem.

### Reflection
> Take a hazard report you've seen written as an outcome ("someone will get hurt"). Rewrite it as a clean hazard statement: hazard → consequence → conditions.

### Knowledge Check — graded • 4 questions • 80% to pass

**Q1. "An operator's hand is crushed in the machine" is best classified as:**
- A) The hazard
- B) The consequence — the harm that results if the hazard is realised
- C) A control
- D) A contributing condition

> **Best answer: B** — The crush is the consequence; the *hazard* is the unguarded pinch point that causes it.

**Q2. Why does confusing hazard with consequence break the SRM process?**
- A) It doesn't — they're the same
- B) You can't control a consequence directly; if you name it as the hazard, every following step aims at the wrong target
- C) It only affects the paperwork
- D) It makes the matrix easier

> **Best answer: B** — You control hazards and conditions; you *prevent* consequences. Name the hazard wrong and the controls become wishes.

**Q3. A contributing condition is:**
- A) The harm itself
- B) Something that makes the hazard more likely to be realised or worse (e.g. poor lighting, time pressure, no training)
- C) The same as the consequence
- D) Always a control

> **Best answer: B** — Conditions raise likelihood or severity; you control them alongside the hazard.

**Q4. "Human error" is a poor hazard statement because:**
- A) Humans never err
- B) It's too vague to score or control — you must push to the specific condition that allows the harm
- C) It's the consequence
- D) It's a control

> **Best answer: B** — Vague statements can't be analysed or controlled. Push to the specific, scorable condition.
