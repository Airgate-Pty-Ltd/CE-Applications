# Module 1 — What MRM Is and Why It Exists

> **Module goal**
>
> Make the learner fluent in the core idea: most serious maintenance-related events are teamwork and communication failures, not skill failures — and MRM is the discipline that addresses the part technical training doesn't. Establish the delta from CRM and the "error chain" idea that runs through the rest of the course.

## Lesson 1.1 — Teamwork Failures, Not Skill Gaps: What MRM Actually Fixes

### Learning objectives
*After this lesson the learner can:*
- Explain why maintenance errors are predominantly teamwork/communication failures rather than competence failures.
- Describe the "error chain" and how MRM works by breaking links, not by demanding perfect humans.
- Frame MRM as the human-factors layer that sits on top of technical competence.

### The teach-out

Here's a sentence I want you to sit with: **the technicians involved in serious maintenance events are, overwhelmingly, competent people.** They knew how to do the job. They'd done it a hundred times. They weren't lazy and they weren't careless in the way that word usually means. So if the skill was there, what failed? The *system around the task* failed — the communication, the handover, the assumption, the missing second look. That is the single most important reframe in this whole course: **maintenance safety is not mainly a skill problem. It's a human-performance problem.** And human-performance problems need human-performance solutions, which is exactly what MRM is.

Let me explain *why* that's true, because it's not obvious. Technical training makes you good at the task. It does not make you immune to the predictable ways humans fail: we forget where we were when we get interrupted, we assume someone else did the part we didn't see, we drift into shortcuts that worked last time, we don't speak up when we're unsure, and we get tired. Notice that *none* of those is a competence gap — a more skilled technician fails in exactly the same ways. They're features of being human. MRM exists because **you cannot train or discipline a human into not being human.** You can only build a system that expects human error and catches it.

That's the second key idea: the **error chain.** Define it plainly: a serious failure is almost never one mistake. It's a *chain* — a sequence of small things that each, alone, would have been harmless. A task gets interrupted. The handover is rushed. The next person assumes it's complete. The independent check gets skipped because everyone's behind. No single link sinks the ship — but link them together, end to end, and you get the in-service event. *Why this matters:* it means you don't need to prevent every error to be safe. **You need to break the chain at any one link.** MRM is, at its heart, a set of tools for putting deliberate break-points into that chain — a structured handover here, an independent inspection there, a culture where "I'm not sure I finished that" gets a thank-you instead of a reprimand.

> **PAUSE & APPLY**
>
> Think about the asymmetry of effort. To *cause* the event, the chain needed every link to line up — interruption, rushed handover, assumption, skipped check. To *prevent* it, you only had to break **one** link. That's the good news buried in human-factors work: the system is fragile to error but it's also full of cheap places to add a defence. MRM is mostly about being deliberate about where you put those defences. You don't need heroics. You need one reliable break-point in the chain.

So where does MRM sit relative to your technical training? On top of it, not instead of it. Technical competence is the floor — you absolutely must know how to do the task. MRM is the layer that protects the task from the predictable human failures that competence alone doesn't touch. *How would you prove an organisation actually has it?* Not by a poster on the wall. By evidence: structured handover records, independent inspection records on critical tasks, error reports that come in freely without people getting punished for them, and a workforce that can tell you, in their own words, when and how they're expected to speak up. *And if it fails?* The chain stays intact, the quiet ground error survives every weak link, and it surfaces — as it always does in maintenance — somewhere downstream, in flight, where it's most expensive to discover.

### Industry example

Dana pulls AeroMach's last three internal occurrence reports and reads them to Priya with the names stripped out. Every one is the same shape: a skilled technician, a normal task, and then a chain — an interruption, a thin handover, an assumption, a check that "everyone was sure was fine so we didn't repeat it." Not one was a person who didn't know the job. Priya's instinct is to ask *who messed up.* Dana stops her: **"Wrong question. Ask where the chain could have been broken — and why our system didn't break it."** That reframe, from blaming the person to auditing the process, is the difference between an organisation that punishes its way to the same event next quarter and one that actually gets safer.

### Reflection
> Recall a maintenance error you've seen or heard about (keep it anonymous). Was the person involved unskilled — or was it a chain of small, normal things lining up? Write the chain out as links. Where could one link have been broken?

### Knowledge Check — graded • 4 questions • 80% to pass

**Q1. Investigations into serious maintenance-related events most often find the root cause is:**
- A) Technicians who lacked the skill to do the task
- B) Teamwork and communication failures around competent people, not a competence gap
- C) Bad luck
- D) Defective tools only

> **Best answer: B** — The people are usually competent; the system around the task fails. MRM addresses that human-performance layer.

**Q2. The "error chain" idea means:**
- A) One huge mistake causes every event
- B) A serious failure is usually a sequence of small, individually harmless errors lining up — and breaking any one link prevents it
- C) Errors can never be prevented
- D) Only the last person in the chain is responsible

> **Best answer: B** — Break the chain at any single link and the event doesn't happen. That's where MRM places its defences.

**Q3. MRM relates to technical training how?**
- A) It replaces technical training
- B) It sits on top of technical competence as the human-factors layer that catches the errors competence alone doesn't prevent
- C) It's only for people who lack technical skill
- D) The two are unrelated

> **Best answer: B** — Competence is the floor; MRM is the protective layer above it.

**Q4. (T/F) Because the technicians involved are usually skilled, the right response to a maintenance error is to find and punish the individual who made it.**
- A) True
- B) False — punishing a skilled person who got caught in a system failure fixes nothing and hides the chain; you audit the process, not the person

> **Best answer: B** — Blame stops the learning. You fix the system that let the chain complete.
