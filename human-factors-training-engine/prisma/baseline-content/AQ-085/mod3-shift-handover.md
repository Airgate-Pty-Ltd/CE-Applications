# Module 3 — The Shift Handover: Maintenance's Highest-Risk Interface

> **Module goal**
>
> Establish the shift handover as *the* critical, high-risk interface in maintenance, teach what a good handover actually contains, and drive home the interrupted-task problem — the half-finished job that changes hands. The learner leaves able to design and critique a structured handover and to recognise the interrupted-task trap.

## Lesson 3.1 — Why the Handover Is the Single Most Dangerous Moment

### Learning objectives
*After this lesson the learner can:*
- Explain why the shift handover concentrates more risk than any other moment in maintenance.
- List what a complete handover must contain (done / open / left-undone / watch-items / status of tools and the task itself).
- Distinguish a structured handover from a verbal end-of-shift "catch-up."

### Competency & Bloom
| Competency | Learning outcome | Bloom's level |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Shift handover | Define and build a complete, structured handover | Understand / Apply |

### The teach-out

If you take one thing from this entire course, take this: **the shift handover is the single most dangerous moment in maintenance.** Not because anything technical is happening — usually nothing is being turned or torqued at all. It's dangerous because it's the moment a task, often half-finished, **changes ownership** — and ownership of a half-finished task is the most fragile thing in the building.

Let me define what a handover actually is, because most people define it far too narrowly. A handover is not "telling the next shift what happened." It's the **transfer of an accurate, shared mental model of the state of the work** from the people leaving to the people arriving — so the incoming team knows exactly what is done, what is not, what was deliberately left, and what to watch. The information is the baton in a relay, and the handover is the moment the baton is passed. Drop it here and the whole race is lost, no matter how well each runner ran their own leg.

*Why is this moment so loaded with risk?* Several forces converge on it at once, and naming them tells you where to defend. **It happens at the worst time** — end of shift, when the outgoing crew is tired and mentally already heading home, and the incoming crew is still spinning up. **It carries the highest-stakes information** — the open tasks, the things that aren't finished, the things that look done but aren't. **It's the prime home of assumption** — the incoming tech assumes a step was done because it usually is; the outgoing tech assumes the incoming tech knows things they don't. And **it's often done verbally, fast, in a corridor** — an open loop at the exact moment you can least afford one. Tired people, the highest-stakes information, maximum assumption, minimum structure. That's not a handover; that's an error chain's favourite starting point.

> **STOP & THINK**
>
> Ask yourself which is more dangerous to hand over: a task that's **finished**, or a task that's **half-finished**? The half-finished one, every time — and it's not close. A finished task has a record and a sign-off. A half-finished task lives partly in the outgoing technician's *head* — "I did this, I hadn't got to that, I was about to check the other thing" — and the instant that head goes home, anything not written down is gone. **The interrupted, half-complete task is the single most failure-prone object in maintenance.** A handover's whole job is to get that fragile mental state out of one head and reliably into another.

So what does a *complete* handover actually contain? Five things, and you should be able to recite them: **(1) what's done and verified** — including sign-offs and records, so it's not just "I did it" but "here's the proof; **(2) what's open** — tasks in progress and exactly how far they got; **(3) what was deliberately left undone** — and *why,* so it isn't mistaken for an oversight; **(4) watch-items** — anything unusual, any concern, anything that didn't feel right; and **(5) the physical state** — the configuration of the work, the status of tools and equipment, what's still removed, what's safetied and what isn't. Miss any one of those and you've handed over a partial picture, which is sometimes worse than no picture, because a partial picture *feels* complete.

The contrast I want burned in: a **structured handover** is deliberate, complete, and ideally written, working through those five categories so nothing rides on memory. A **verbal catch-up at the door** is a tired person summarising from memory to another tired person who's half-listening — and it's where parts get left loose, steps get assumed done, and "I thought you knew about that" is born. *How would you prove an organisation handles handovers well?* Structured handover records exist and are actually used; open tasks are documented with their exact state; there's a defined process, not a corridor conversation; and incoming staff can reliably tell you the true state of the work they inherited. *And if it fails?* The most common and most quietly catastrophic maintenance failure of all: a step that nobody did, because each shift believed the other had.

### Industry example

A panel job at AeroMach is two-thirds done when the day shift ends: most fasteners torqued and stamped, two not yet done, the duplicate inspection not yet performed. The outgoing tech, halfway out the door, says "panel's basically done, just close it up" — and leaves. That sentence is the whole disaster in miniature: it transferred a *feeling* ("basically done") instead of a *state* (two fasteners untorqued, no duplicate check). The night shift, taking "basically done" at face value, closes the panel. The two untorqued fasteners and the missing inspection are now sealed inside, invisible — and they'll stay invisible right up until the moment in flight when they aren't. Dana's fix isn't to discipline anyone. It's to make a structured handover mandatory for any open task: done-and-verified, open, left-undone, watch, physical state — written, every time. **"Basically done" is not a handover. It's an error chain with a friendly face.**

### Reflection
> Think of the last task you handed over (or received) that wasn't 100% finished. Did the handover cover all five categories — done/open/left-undone/watch/physical state? Which one was missing or weakest?

## Lesson 3.2 — The Interrupted-Task Problem (and Designing the Fix)

### Learning objectives
*After this lesson the learner can:*
- Explain why an interrupted task is uniquely prone to a missed step, with or without a shift change.
- Apply defences against the interrupted-task trap (mark the stop-point, document state, re-verify on resume).
- Build a simple structured-handover format usable in their own area.

### Competency & Bloom
| Competency | Learning outcome | Bloom's level |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Error capture | Defend against the interrupted-task failure mode | Apply |

### The teach-out

Let me isolate the mechanism behind so many handover failures, because once you see it you'll spot it everywhere: the **interrupted task.** Define it simply — an interrupted task is any job that gets paused before it's complete, whether by a shift change, a phone call, a parts wait, a higher-priority job, or just a coffee break. And here's the human-factors fact that makes it dangerous: **when we're interrupted mid-task, we are remarkably bad at knowing exactly where we stopped — and even worse at proving we did the step right before the one we stopped on.**

*Why does this happen?* Because of how memory works under interruption. When you resume a paused task, your brain wants to feel like it's *continuing,* not *restarting* — so it tends to assume the step just before the stop-point was completed, even when it wasn't. You torque five of six fasteners, you get pulled away, you come back, and your mind quietly tells you "I was on the last one" — when in fact you'd only done four and were *reaching* for the fifth. The interruption erased the boundary between "done" and "about to do," and the missed fastener is now invisible to the very person who missed it. This is not carelessness. It's a known, predictable feature of human cognition under interruption — which is exactly why we don't fight it with willpower, we fight it with **process.**

Now layer the shift change on top. An ordinary interruption is *one person* misremembering where they stopped. A shift-change interruption is *one person's fragile memory of the stop-point being transferred to a completely different person* — who has zero firsthand memory of the task at all. The incoming tech has nothing to misremember *with;* they have only what the handover told them. So the interrupted task and the handover failure are the same problem at two scales, and they stack: the worst case in all of maintenance is an **interrupted critical task handed across a shift boundary on a vague verbal note.** That single sentence describes a frighteningly large share of serious maintenance events.

> **STOP & THINK**
>
> Here's the defence in one move: **never trust the resume — verify it.** Whenever you return to an interrupted task — after a break, a phone call, or a shift handover — do not assume you (or the previous person) finished the last step. Go back one step. Physically confirm the state. "Where did this actually stop?" is a far safer question than "where do I *remember* it stopping?" The handover note exists to make that re-verification possible; the discipline of actually re-verifying is what closes the trap.

So how do you design the fix? Three practical defences, and they're cheap. **First, mark the stop-point physically** when you pause a task — a flag, a tag, an incomplete-work marker on the actual hardware, not just in your head. **Second, document the state** — write down what's done and what's open *at the moment you stop,* while your memory is fresh, not at the end of the shift when it's gone. **Third, re-verify on resume** — whoever picks the task back up, including yourself, treats the resume point as suspect and physically confirms the last completed step before continuing. Build those three into your handover format and you've turned the most failure-prone object in maintenance into a managed one. *How do you prove it's working?* Incomplete-work markers are visible on paused jobs; handover notes capture state at the point of interruption; and re-verification on resume is an expected, observable step. *And if it fails?* The missed step survives the interruption, survives the handover, survives close-up — and waits.

### Industry example

AeroMach builds a one-page **structured handover card** after the panel incident, and it's deliberately boring: a job ID; a "done-and-verified" box with sign-off; an "open / in-progress — stopped at exactly this step" box; a "deliberately left undone, and why" box; a "watch / concerns" box; and a "physical state — tools, removed parts, what's safetied" box. They pair it with a rule: any task paused mid-step gets an **incomplete-work tag** on the hardware itself. The first week, Ray's night shift inherits a paused job whose card reads "stopped after fastener 4 of 6 — fasteners 5 and 6 NOT done, NOT inspected," with a tag hanging on the panel. Ray's team re-verifies fastener 4, completes 5 and 6, does the duplicate inspection, and signs off — no assumption, no gap. **The boring card did what a brilliant memory could not: it carried the exact stop-point across a shift change.**

### Reflection
> Sketch a structured-handover card for *your* work: what boxes would it need? Include a way to mark the stop-point on an interrupted task and a re-verify-on-resume step. You'll build this for real in the practical.

### Knowledge Check — graded • 4 questions • 80% to pass

**Q1. An interrupted task is uniquely dangerous because:**
- A) The work is always restarted from scratch
- B) When we resume, our memory tends to assume the step before the stop-point was completed — even when it wasn't — so the missed step is invisible to the person who missed it
- C) Interruptions never happen in maintenance
- D) It only matters across shifts

> **Best answer: B** — Interruption erases the "done vs about-to-do" boundary. It's a cognitive feature, not carelessness.

**Q2. The worst-case combination in maintenance is:**
- A) A finished task documented and signed off
- B) An interrupted critical task handed across a shift boundary on a vague verbal note
- C) A task not yet started
- D) A task done by one person start to finish

> **Best answer: B** — The interrupted-task trap and the handover trap stack on top of each other.

**Q3. The single best defence when resuming an interrupted task is to:**
- A) Trust your memory of where you stopped
- B) Treat the resume point as suspect — go back one step and physically verify the last completed step before continuing
- C) Start over completely every time
- D) Skip the step you think you finished

> **Best answer: B** — Verify the resume, don't trust it. "Where did it actually stop?" beats "where do I remember stopping?"

**Q4. Three practical defences against the interrupted-task trap are:**
- A) Work faster, skip breaks, rely on memory
- B) Mark the stop-point physically, document the state at the moment of interruption, and re-verify on resume
- C) Hand over verbally, hope, and move on
- D) Punish whoever forgets

> **Best answer: B** — Cheap, physical, process-based defences beat willpower every time.
