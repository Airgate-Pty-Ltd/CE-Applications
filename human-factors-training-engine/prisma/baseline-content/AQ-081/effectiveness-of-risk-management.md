# Module 2 — Safety Performance Monitoring and Measurement

> **Module goal**
>
> Teach the learner to watch the system honestly: monitor the system and its processes (not just outcomes), read trends rather than single points, and judge data quality before trusting a number. They leave able to interpret a real trend and to distrust a clean-looking number for the right reasons.

## Lesson 2.1 — Monitor the System, Not Just the Outcomes

### Learning Objectives
*After this lesson the learner can:*
- Distinguish monitoring outcomes from monitoring the system and its processes.
- Explain why a clean outcome can hide a sick process.
- List the main data sources a safety performance monitoring program draws on.

### Competency & Bloom
| Competency | Learning outcome | Bloom's level |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Performance monitoring | Monitor process health, not only end results | Understand / Apply |

### The Teach-Out

Let me define **safety performance monitoring and measurement** plainly: it's the ongoing collection and review of safety data to judge whether the organization is achieving its safety performance — and, just as important, whether the *processes* that produce safety are healthy. The trap I want to keep you out of is monitoring only the **outcomes** — the accidents, the injuries, the escapes — and calling that a safety program.

Here's why outcomes alone aren't enough. Outcomes are *lagging* — they happen at the end, after the failure. And in a well-run, lower-volume, high-consequence world like AeroMach's, serious outcomes are mercifully rare, which means if you only watch outcomes you're watching a number that's usually zero. A flat line of zero accidents tells you almost nothing about whether your processes are about to produce one. **A clean outcome can sit on top of a very sick process.** The torque step can be drifting, the second sign-offs can be getting skipped, the reports can be drying up — and the accident column still reads zero, right up until it doesn't.

So strong monitoring watches the **system and its processes**, not just the scoreboard. It asks: Are people reporting hazards at a healthy rate? Are corrective actions getting done on time, or piling up overdue? Are the controls we rely on actually being executed? Are audit findings trending up or down? Those are *process* signals, and they move *before* the outcome does. Watching them is the difference between an early warning and a post-mortem.

> **PAUSE & APPLY**
>
> Where does the data come from? A real monitoring program pulls from many streams, not one: hazard and event reports, investigation findings, audit and evaluation results, the status of corrective actions, the indicators we'll build in Module 3, change records, and operational data (inspections, test results, defect rates). One stream lies to you easily; many streams cross-check each other. At AeroMach, when the reports went quiet but the overdue-actions list climbed, the *combination* told the true story that either number alone would have hidden.

A discipline to carry: **read the signal honestly, even when it's inconvenient.** The most valuable thing a monitoring program produces isn't a green dashboard — it's an uncomfortable question asked early. If your monitoring never makes anyone uncomfortable, it's probably measuring the wrong things, or measuring them to be reassured rather than informed.

### Industry Example

AeroMach's old "safety report" was one number: lost-time injuries, which were near zero, so every report said "safety is good." Priya rebuilt it to watch the system: reporting rate by department, percentage of corrective actions overdue, control-execution sampling on the flight-critical steps, and audit-finding trend. The injury number stayed near zero — but the new view immediately showed two departments that had stopped reporting entirely and a backlog of overdue safety actions in overhaul. **The outcome had been hiding the process the whole time.** Nobody had been lying; they'd just been measuring the one thing least likely to warn them.

### Reflection
> Name one *outcome* metric your organization watches (injuries, escapes, events). Now name one *process* signal that would move *before* that outcome did. Which one are you actually watching today?

## Lesson 2.2 — Trends, Data Quality, and the "Watched but Never Acted On" Failure

### Learning Objectives
*After this lesson the learner can:*
- Explain why a trend (direction over time) carries more information than a single data point.
- Judge data quality and name what makes a number untrustworthy.
- Describe the "watched but never acted on" failure and why measuring without acting is worthless.

### Competency & Bloom
| Competency | Learning outcome | Bloom's level |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Trending & data quality | Read trends, test data quality, insist on action | Understand / Apply |

### The Teach-Out

Three things separate monitoring that protects you from monitoring that just generates charts: you read **trends**, you check **data quality**, and you **act** on what you see. Let me take them in turn.

**Trends.** Define it simply: a trend is *direction over time.* One data point is noise — you genuinely cannot tell anything from it. Three points heading the same way is information. The whole reason to monitor continuously rather than glance once a year is so you can see direction: is this getting better, worse, or holding? A *rising* trend of the same near-miss is the system warning you, in advance, about the accident it's building toward. Trend by area, too — an organization-wide average can look flat while one department is on fire, because the good areas mask the bad one. Trend locally so you can act locally.

**Data quality.** A trend is only as honest as the data under it, so before you trust a number, interrogate it. Is it *complete* — or are whole areas not reporting? Is it *consistent* — is everyone counting the same thing the same way, or does "near-miss" mean different things to different supervisors? Is it *honest* — is there pressure that quietly discourages reporting bad news? Is it *current* — or are you steering off last quarter's picture? A beautiful trend built on bad data is worse than no trend, because it's confidently wrong. The classic trap, again: a number that *improved* because people stopped reporting, not because performance improved.

> **STOP & THINK**
>
> Here's the failure that wastes more assurance effort than any other: **"watched but never acted on."** An organization stands up a gorgeous dashboard, reviews it every month, notes the concerning trend… and does nothing. The indicator went red, everyone saw it, and no action followed. That is *worse* than not measuring, because now you've documented that you saw the warning and ignored it — which is exactly what an investigator will find later. **An indicator with no action attached is decoration.** The value of monitoring is not the watching; it's the acting.

So tie it together: a trend tells you direction; data quality tells you whether to believe the trend; and the only thing that makes any of it matter is that a red trend *triggers a response* — re-assess the risk, fix the control, escalate to management. Measurement without action isn't assurance. It's a very organized way of being surprised.

### Industry Example

AeroMach's overhaul shop had a slowly rising trend of a particular seal defect — visible on Priya's dashboard for four straight months. It was reviewed in every monthly meeting. Nobody owned an action, so nothing happened; the trend was "noted." In month five the defect caused a return-to-service escape. The investigation pulled the dashboard and found the rising line, reviewed and un-actioned, month after month. Dana's hardest lesson from that: the dashboard hadn't failed — *the organization had watched the warning and never acted.* AeroMach now has a rule: any indicator that crosses its trigger gets a named owner and a due date before the meeting ends, or the meeting isn't over.

### Reflection
> Pick a safety number your organization reports. Score it honestly on completeness, consistency, honesty, and currency. Then ask the harder question: the last time it moved the wrong way, did a named person own an action by the time the meeting ended?

### Knowledge Check — graded • 4 questions • 80% to pass

**Q1. A single data point versus a trend:**
- A) A single point is more reliable than a trend
- B) A single point is noise; a trend (direction over time) carries the information
- C) They are equivalent
- D) Trends should be avoided

> **Best answer: B** — One point tells you almost nothing; direction over time is the signal.

**Q2. You should trend safety data by area (department/process), not just organization-wide, because:**
- A) It looks more impressive
- B) An organization-wide average can look flat while one area is failing — the good areas mask the bad one
- C) Regulators forbid averages
- D) Local data is always wrong

> **Best answer: B** — Average masks local fires. Trend locally so you can act locally.

**Q3. Before trusting a safety metric you should check that it is:**
- A) Green
- B) Complete, consistent, honest, and current — bad data makes a confident-looking but wrong trend
- C) Approved by management
- D) Higher than last year

**Q4. The "watched but never acted on" failure means:**
- A) The dashboard was too detailed
- B) The organization saw a concerning indicator and took no action — which is worse than not measuring, because the ignored warning is now on record

- D) Too many people acted

> **Best answer: B** — An indicator with no action attached is decoration. The value is in the acting, not the watching.

# Module 3 — Safety Performance Indicators (SPIs) and Targets (SPTs)

> **Module goal**
>
> The heart of the course. The learner leaves able to define SPIs and SPTs, distinguish leading from lagging indicators, build an indicator that's actually meaningful (specific, measurable, owned, with a trigger), and avoid the classic indicator failures (vanity metrics, gameable counts, targets that drive the wrong behavior).

## Lesson 3.1 — SPIs and SPTs, Leading vs Lagging

### Learning Objectives
*After this lesson the learner can:*
- Define a Safety Performance Indicator (SPI) and a Safety Performance Target (SPT).
- Distinguish leading from lagging indicators and give examples of each.
- Explain why a healthy indicator set is mostly leading, steered by, but not limited to, lagging.

### Competency & Bloom
| Competency | Learning outcome | Bloom's level |
| --- | --- | --- |
| SPIs / SPTs | Define indicators and targets; classify leading vs lagging | Understand / Apply |

### The Teach-Out

This is the section people remember the longest, so let me define the two terms cleanly before the initials.

A **Safety Performance Indicator — an SPI** — is a *measure you've chosen to track because it tells you something about safety performance.* It's the thing you watch: reporting rate, overdue-action count, escape rate, audit findings, training currency. An SPI by itself is just a number on a line.

A **Safety Performance Target — an SPT** — is *the value you're aiming that indicator at.* It turns the indicator into a goal: not just "track overdue corrective actions," but "keep overdue corrective actions below five percent." The SPI is the gauge; the SPT is the line you've drawn on the gauge. Indicators without targets drift, because there's no defined "good"; targets without indicators are wishes, because there's nothing measuring them. You need both.

Now the distinction that runs through the whole pillar: **leading versus lagging.**

A **lagging indicator** measures something that has *already happened* — an event, an injury, an escape, a repeat finding. It's the scoreboard. Lagging indicators are real and necessary — they're the ground truth of whether harm occurred — but they have one fatal limit: by the time a lagging indicator moves, the bad thing has *already* happened. You're reading history.

A **leading indicator** measures something that warns you *before* harm occurs — the reporting rate (is the safety pipeline alive?), the percentage of overdue safety actions (is the backlog growing?), audit-finding trends (are gaps appearing?), training currency (are people qualified?), control-execution sampling (are the controls actually being done?). Leading indicators move *upstream* of the outcome. They're the early-warning system.

> **PAUSE & APPLY**
>
> Sort these fast — it's exactly the muscle the quiz tests. *Lagging:* number of in-service failures, lost-time injuries, customer escapes, repeat audit findings. *Leading:* hazard reporting rate, percent of corrective actions overdue, percent of staff with current safety training, control-execution audit pass rate, near-miss reporting trend. The test for "leading": **does this number move before someone gets hurt?** If yes, it's leading. If it only moves after, it's lagging.

Here's the strategy to carry out of this lesson. A healthy indicator set is **mostly leading, anchored by a few lagging.** You keep the lagging ones because they're the honest scoreboard — they keep you from fooling yourself. But you *manage* by the leading ones, because those are the only ones you can still do something about. We'll spend the next lesson on what makes an individual indicator actually good — because a leading indicator badly built is just as useless as a lagging one.

### Industry Example

AeroMach's first "SPIs" were all lagging: in-service failures, escapes, injuries — all near zero, all reassuring, all useless for prevention because by the time any of them moved, the harm was done. Priya rebuilt the set around leading indicators: hazard reporting rate (with a target floor, not a ceiling — they *wanted* reports up), percent of flight-critical control checks actually executed, percent of corrective actions overdue, and training currency on the critical tasks. She kept two lagging indicators as the honest scoreboard. For the first time, AeroMach had numbers that moved *before* an event — numbers Dana could actually act on while there was still time.

### Reflection
> List three numbers your organization currently calls "safety metrics." Classify each as leading or lagging. If they're all lagging, you've just found your biggest assurance gap.

> **Best answer: B** — The SPI is the gauge; the SPT is the line drawn on it. You need both.

## Lesson 3.2 — Building an Indicator That Actually Means Something

### Learning Objectives
*After this lesson the learner can:*
- List what makes an SPI meaningful: tied to a real risk/control, specific, measurable, owned, with a target and a trigger.
- Set a sensible SPT (including direction) and a trigger that forces action.
- Recognize and avoid vanity metrics, gameable counts, and targets that drive the wrong behavior.

### Competency & Bloom
| Competency | Learning outcome | Bloom's level |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Indicator design | Build a meaningful SPI/SPT with owner and trigger | Apply |

### The Teach-Out

Knowing leading-versus-lagging is the easy half. The hard half is building an indicator that *actually means something* — because a badly built indicator is worse than none. Here's the anatomy of a good SPI; an indicator that's missing any of these pieces is decoration.

**It's tied to a real risk or control.** Every meaningful SPI exists to answer "is *this specific* risk staying controlled?" If you can't name the hazard or control behind the number, you've got a metric in search of a meaning. The flight-critical torque control gets an indicator *because* that risk would hurt someone if the control slipped. Don't measure what's easy; measure what matters.

**It's specific and measurable.** "Improve safety culture" is not an indicator. "Hazard reports per 100 staff per month" is. If two reasonable people would count it differently, it's not specific enough yet. Define the numerator, the denominator, and the period so the number means the same thing every time.

**It has a target (SPT) with a direction.** Decide what "good" looks like *and which way you want it to move.* This is where people get burned: for an escape rate, lower is better; but for *reporting* rate, **higher is better** — a rising report count usually means a healthier reporting culture, not a more dangerous operation. Set the target to reward the behavior you actually want.

**It has a trigger and an owner.** Decide in advance: at what value does this indicator demand action, who owns that action, and what's the response? An SPI with no trigger is the "watched but never acted on" failure waiting to happen. The trigger turns a chart into a control.

> **STOP & THINK**
>
> Three indicator traps, because all three are common and all three are dangerous:
>
> **The vanity metric** — a number that always looks good and never drives a decision (e.g., "number of safety posters displayed"). It's there to reassure, not to inform. Cut it.
>
> **The gameable count** — an indicator people can improve by *changing the counting*, not the safety (e.g., a raw "incident count" that drops because people reclassify incidents as "non-events" to keep it low). If the easiest way to hit the target is to fiddle the definition, the indicator is broken.
>
> **The perverse target** — a target that drives the *wrong* behavior. Set a target of "zero reported hazards" and you won't get zero hazards; you'll get zero *reports*, because you've just told people that reporting is failure. The number will look perfect while the operation goes blind. **Be very careful what you reward — you'll get it.**

So the build, every time: name the risk or control → write a specific, measurable number → set a target with the right direction → set a trigger and name an owner. Do that and you've built an indicator that protects the organization. Skip any step and you've built a chart.

### Industry Example

Priya's first draft SPI for AeroMach was "number of safety meetings held" — easy to count, always green, and completely meaningless: it never once told anyone whether a control was working. A textbook vanity metric. She replaced it with: *"Percent of flight-critical torque sign-offs verified present and complete on a weekly sample"* — tied to a real control, specific (sample of 20 per week), measurable, target ≥ 98%, trigger at < 95% goes to Dana with a 5-day action, owner named. The week the figure dropped to 90%, the trigger fired, the action found a new operator skipping the second sign-off, and the control was fixed *before* a defect escaped. **That** is what an indicator is for. The meeting-count never could have done it.

### Reflection
> Take the control you've been carrying since Module 0. Draft one SPI for it: name the number, the target (and which direction is "good"), the trigger value, and the owner. You'll bring this to the practical.

> **Best answer: B** — Risk/control + specific + measurable + target + trigger + owner. Miss one and it's decoration.

**Q3. A "vanity metric" is:**

> **Best answer: B** — Be careful what you reward; a perverse target drives the wrong behavior.

# Module 4 — Internal Safety Audits and Evaluations

> **Module goal**
>
> Teach the learner to audit the SMS itself — requirement vs objective evidence vs gap — applied to safety processes and the controls SRM produced. Cover what's distinctive about auditing an SMS, the "audit the process not the person" discipline, and effectiveness evaluation: did the fix actually hold over time.

## Lesson 4.1 — Auditing the SMS Itself

### Learning Objectives
*After this lesson the learner can:*
- Explain that internal safety audit examines the SMS and its controls, not just compliance paperwork.
- Apply the requirement → objective evidence → gap structure to a safety finding.
- Apply "audit the process, not the person" and "show me the objective evidence" to a safety audit.

### Competency & Bloom
| Competency | Learning outcome | Bloom's level |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Internal safety audit | Audit the SMS using requirement–evidence–gap | Apply |

### The Teach-Out

You may already audit quality systems; auditing an SMS uses the same engine, pointed at a different target. Let me define the target first. An **internal safety audit or evaluation** is a deliberate, planned examination of the SMS — its processes *and* the safety controls those processes produced — to confirm they exist, are being followed, and are actually working. Notice the difference from a pure compliance audit: you're not only asking "does a procedure exist and was the box ticked"; you're asking "**is this control actually doing its safety job?**"

The engine is the one I teach in every auditing course, and it doesn't change: **requirement → objective evidence → gap.** Start with the *requirement* — what your own SMS says should happen (the procedure, the control, the indicator response). Then go find the *objective evidence* — what actually happens on the floor: records, samples, observations, re-performance. The *gap* between the two is your finding. No requirement, nothing to audit against; no objective evidence, you're auditing opinions; no gap, no finding. Simple, and it works on any system.

Two of my standing rules apply with extra force in safety auditing. First, **"show me the objective evidence."** A manager telling you "yes, we always do the second sign-off" is not evidence — it's a claim. Evidence is the last twenty sign-off records, a live observation of the step, a re-performance. In safety, the gap between what people *believe* happens and what *actually* happens is exactly where the quietly-dead control lives. Your job is to go look.

> **PAUSE & APPLY**
>
> What makes auditing an SMS distinctive? You audit three layers, not one. **The process** (does the SMS process exist and meet its intent?), **the records** (is it being followed?), and — the one people skip — **the control's actual effect** (is the risk genuinely being held down, or just being managed on paper?). At AeroMach, Sam's audit of the actuator functional-test control didn't stop at "is there a procedure" (yes) and "are there records" (yes). He asked the third question — *is the test actually catching defective units?* — and found the fixture had been broken for months. The first two layers passed. The third caught the dead control.

Second rule: **audit the process, not the person.** When you find a skipped sign-off, the finding is *not* "Operator X is careless." The finding is "the control allows the second sign-off to be skipped without detection." Blame the person and you'll learn nothing and they'll hide the next problem; examine the process and you'll fix the thing that let it happen — and keep people willing to tell you the truth, which is the lifeblood of the whole SMS. A safety audit that makes people defensive has destroyed the very reporting culture Pillar 3 depends on.

### Industry Example

Sam audited AeroMach's management-of-change control. Requirement: every significant change runs through a safety review before implementation. Objective evidence: he pulled the last fifteen changes — eleven had a documented safety review, four didn't. Gap: the control was being bypassed for changes that someone judged "minor." He wrote it as a *process* finding — "the MoC trigger has no enforcement; significant changes can proceed without the required review" — not as "the engineers ignored the rule." That framing got him an honest conversation about *why* the four slipped (no clear definition of 'significant'), which fixed the actual hole. Blame would have gotten him silence and four more bypassed changes.

### Reflection
> Pick one safety control in your organization. Write the *requirement* (what your system says should happen), then the *objective evidence* you'd go and get to prove it's working. If you can't name the evidence, the control isn't auditable yet — and that's a finding in itself.

### Knowledge Check — graded • 4 questions • 80% to pass

**Q1. An internal safety audit differs from a pure compliance check because it also asks:**
- A) Whether the binder is neat
- B) Whether the control is actually doing its safety job — not just whether a procedure exists and a box was ticked
- C) Who to blame
- D) Whether the auditor is liked

> **Best answer: B** — Audit the control's real effect, not just the paperwork's existence.

**Q2. The structure of a safety audit finding is:**
- A) Opinion → blame → corrective action
- B) Requirement → objective evidence → gap
- C) Severity → likelihood → risk
- D) Hazard → control → residual

> **Best answer: B** — What should happen, what actually happens, the difference. The difference is the finding.

**Q3. "Show me the objective evidence" matters in safety auditing because:**
- A) Auditors enjoy paperwork
- B) The gap between what people *believe* happens and what *actually* happens is exactly where a quietly-failed control hides — a verbal claim is not evidence
- C) Records are required by marketing
- D) It speeds up the audit

> **Best answer: B** — Belief isn't evidence. Go look, sample, re-perform.

**Q4. "Audit the process, not the person" protects the SMS because:**
- A) People are never at fault
- B) Blaming individuals teaches you nothing, hides the real process gap, and destroys the reporting culture the whole SMS depends on
- C) Processes can't fail
- D) It's faster

> **Best answer: B** — Examine the process that allowed the failure; keep people willing to tell the truth.

## Lesson 4.2 — Effectiveness Evaluation: Did the Fix Actually Hold?

### Learning Objectives
*After this lesson the learner can:*
- Define effectiveness evaluation and distinguish "closed" from "effective."
- State, up front, what evidence over what period would prove a corrective action held.
- Explain why a closed corrective-action form is not proof a problem was solved.

### Competency & Bloom
| Competency | Learning outcome | Bloom's level |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Effectiveness evaluation | Verify a corrective action held over time | Apply |

### The Teach-Out

Here's the step almost everyone skips, and it's the difference between an SMS that improves and one that just churns paper: **effectiveness evaluation.** Define it plainly: effectiveness evaluation is *checking, after a corrective action has been implemented, whether it actually fixed the problem — and whether the fix stayed fixed over time.*

The trap is the word "closed." A corrective action gets "closed" the moment the action is *done* — the procedure was updated, the training was delivered, the fixture was repaired. But "done" is not "worked." A closed form tells you somebody *acted*; it tells you nothing about whether the action actually removed the cause and held. The most common safety self-deception is a wall of green "closed" corrective actions sitting on top of problems that quietly came back. **No effectiveness check, no real closure — just a closed form.**

So how do you do it right? You decide, *up front, before you close it,* two things: **what evidence would prove this worked, and over what period.** "We retrained the operators" closes the form. "Over the next three months, the second-sign-off sampling stays above 98% and the related defect doesn't recur" is an effectiveness check — it names the evidence and the window. You set that target when you take the action, then you actually come back and look. If the evidence shows up over the period, *now* it's effective. If it doesn't, the action failed and you escalate — back into SRM to redesign the control.

> **STOP & THINK**
>
> Two different questions, and people constantly merge them. *"Is it closed?"* asks: did we do the action? *"Is it effective?"* asks: did the action work, and is it still working? An action can be closed and ineffective at the same time — that's the most dangerous combination in the whole pillar, because the green form actively tells you to stop worrying about a problem that isn't solved. Decide what "effective" looks like **before** you let yourself close it.

This is also where Pillar 3 loops back into Pillar 2, which we'll make explicit in the next module: if a corrective action *fails* its effectiveness check, that's not a closed item — it's a live signal that the risk was never actually controlled, and it goes straight back into Safety Risk Management for a new control. Effectiveness evaluation is the tripwire that catches a fix before the floor does.

### Industry Example

After the seal-defect escape, AeroMach's corrective action was "revise the inspection instruction and retrain." Old AeroMach would have closed it the day training finished — green form, problem "solved." New AeroMach, with Priya's discipline, defined the effectiveness check up front: *the seal-defect trend stays at zero for three months and the inspection-step sampling stays above 95%.* They closed the *action* but kept the *effectiveness check* open. In month two, the defect reappeared once — the effectiveness check failed, which told them the retraining hadn't actually fixed the root cause. It went back into SRM, where they found the real cause was a tooling issue training could never fix. The effectiveness check caught a "solved" problem that wasn't.

### Reflection
> Find one corrective action your organization closed recently. Was an *effectiveness check* defined — specific evidence over a specific period — or did "action complete" equal "closed"? If the latter, how would you know if the problem came back?

### Knowledge Check — graded • 4 questions • 80% to pass

**Q1. Effectiveness evaluation asks:**
- A) Was the corrective-action form signed?
- B) Did the action actually fix the problem — and did the fix stay fixed over time?
- C) Was everyone busy?
- D) Was the action done on time?

> **Best answer: B** — Done is not the same as worked. Effectiveness asks whether it worked and held.

**Q2. A "closed" corrective action proves:**
- A) The problem is permanently solved
- B) Only that the action was completed — not that it removed the cause or held over time
- C) The control is effective
- D) Nothing needs follow-up

> **Best answer: B** — Closed means acted, not solved. The green form can sit on a problem that came back.

**Q3. You should decide what evidence proves an action worked:**
- A) Only after the problem recurs
- B) Up front, before closing it — naming the specific evidence and the period over which it must hold
- C) Never — it's the auditor's job
- D) Whenever convenient

> **Best answer: B** — Define the effectiveness target when you take the action, then come back and verify.

**Q4. When a corrective action fails its effectiveness check, the right move is:**
- A) Close it anyway — the action was done
- B) Treat it as a live signal that the risk was never controlled, and feed it back into SRM for a new control
- C) Blame the operator
- D) Lower the target

> **Best answer: B** — A failed effectiveness check is the tripwire that sends a problem back to Pillar 2.
