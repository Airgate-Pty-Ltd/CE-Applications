**LAYER B — TEACH THE SUBJECT**

### 7. The teach-out

Let me start with the question that sits under almost every serious safety failure I've ever investigated: *why did a competent, careful, well-trained person do that?* Not a lazy person. Not an untrained person. A good person, on a normal day, who did something that — afterward — looks obviously wrong. If you've ever read an incident report and thought "how could anyone miss that?", you've already met the problem this whole course exists to answer.

The answer is **human factors**. Let me define it before we lean on the term for the rest of the course. Human factors is **the study of how human performance — attention, memory, perception, judgement, communication — actually behaves under real working conditions, and how the design of work, tools, procedures, and teams either fights human limits or works with them.** In plain terms: it's the science of why good people make predictable mistakes, and what you do about it.

Here's the foundational idea, and I want you to carry it through every lesson: **error is normal.** It is not a sign of a bad worker or a moral failing. Human attention is a narrow spotlight, not a floodlight. Human memory drops things, especially when interrupted. Human judgement takes shortcuts under pressure. These aren't defects in particular people — they're features of *every* human nervous system, including yours and mine. A person who has never made an error has simply not done enough work yet.

> **STOP & THINK**
>
> If error is normal — built into how people work — then "be more careful" is not a safety control. It's a wish. Hold that thought. Almost everything in this course is about replacing the wish ("try harder, pay more attention, don't make mistakes") with an actual control (design the work so the error is harder to make and easier to catch). The day you stop being surprised that good people err is the day you start preventing it.

Why does this matter so much that some practitioners call human factors the single most important supporting course in a safety program? Because every other safety discipline — your procedures, your inspections, your audits, your corrective actions — is ultimately *carried out by humans*. If you don't understand how human performance bends and breaks, your beautiful system still fails at the one point you didn't engineer for: the person. Regulators across the major aviation authorities expect human-factors competence precisely because they learned, the hard way, that you cannot have a safe operation while pretending people are machines.

A word on how the course runs, so there are no surprises. Seven sections. We start with what human factors *is* and why error is normal. Then the **SHELL model** — a simple map of the person and everything they have to interface with. Then **error types** — the different ways competent people err, because slips, lapses, mistakes, and violations are not the same thing and don't have the same fix. Then **performance-shaping factors** — fatigue, stress, workload, time pressure, complacency — the conditions that make error more likely. Then the **Swiss-Cheese model** — how a single front-line error is almost never the whole story, and where latent failures hide. And finally **error management and just culture** — how you actually prevent, catch, and recover from error, and why blame quietly makes you *less* safe. Each section ends in a graded check at eighty percent; the course ends with a final exam at eighty percent plus a short practical where you analyse a real AeroMach error event.

And the reassurance that pairs with the bar: **if you attend the whole course and engage with AeroMach's situations, you should not have a problem.** The bar is set high because that's what makes the certificate mean something. Clearing it is the point.

### 8. Industry example

At AeroMach, Dana the Safety Manager spent years doing what most organizations do after an error: find the person, retrain the person, write them up, move on. And the same kinds of errors kept happening — different people, same patterns. The day she reframed it — "stop asking *who* failed and start asking *what conditions made this error likely*" — the recurring problems finally started to fall. That reframe *is* human factors. Priya, the new safety officer, is about to learn in a week what took Dana years of repeated escapes to figure out. That's the stake: this isn't theory, it's the difference between an organization that keeps blaming and re-blaming, and one that actually gets safer.

### 14. Reflection

### 15. Knowledge Check — graded • 3 questions • 80% to pass

**Q1. Human factors is best described as:**
- A) A way to identify which workers are careless
- B) The study of how human performance behaves under real conditions, and how work design works with or against human limits
- C) A type of mechanical failure analysis
- D) A disciplinary process

> **Best answer: B** — It's the science of why good people make predictable errors and what to do about it — not a tool for blaming individuals.

**Q2. The foundational idea of this course is that error is:**
- A) A sign of a bad or untrained worker
- B) Normal — a feature of how every human's attention, memory, and judgement work, not a moral failing
- C) Rare and avoidable with enough willpower
- D) Only a problem for new employees

> **Best answer: B** — Error is normal. Accepting that is what lets you design controls instead of issuing wishes.

**Q3. "Be more careful" is not considered a real safety control because:**
- A) It costs too much
- B) Error is built into normal human performance, so willpower can't reliably prevent it — you need to design the work to prevent and catch error
- C) Workers ignore instructions
- D) It's against regulations

> **Best answer: B** — If error is normal, "try harder" is a wish. Real controls change the conditions, not just the exhortation.
