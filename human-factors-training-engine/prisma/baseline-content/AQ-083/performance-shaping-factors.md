# Section 3 — Performance-Shaping Factors: The Conditions That Make Error Likely

> **Section goal**
>
> Teach the conditions that bend human performance — fatigue, stress, workload, time pressure, and complacency — as *manageable factors*, not fixed traits. The learner leaves able to spot a performance-shaping factor in a situation and treat it as a controllable hazard rather than a personal weakness.

**LAYER B — TEACH THE SUBJECT**

### 7. The teach-out

We've established that error is normal. Now let's talk about the conditions that make it *more* normal — the things that turn a perfectly capable person into a predictably error-prone one. Let me define the family before naming examples. A **performance-shaping factor** is any condition — internal or external — that raises or lowers the likelihood of human error. They don't *cause* the error directly; they load the dice. And crucially, **they are conditions you can manage**, not character traits you're stuck with. Let me take the heavy hitters.

**Fatigue.** *Define it:* a state of reduced physical and mental capability from insufficient or poor-quality rest, extended wakefulness, or working against the body clock. *Why it's so dangerous:* fatigue degrades exactly the abilities precision work depends on — attention, working memory, reaction time, and judgement — to a degree comparable with other well-known impairments. And here's the cruel part: **the fatigued person is the worst possible judge of their own fatigue.** You don't feel your judgement slipping; you feel fine while making worse decisions. That's why fatigue can't be managed by asking people if they're tired. It's managed by *scheduling* — sane rosters, limits on consecutive shifts, attention to night work and the body clock.

**Stress.** *Define it:* the body's response to demand. A little stress *sharpens* performance — that's useful. Too much *narrows* it: attention tunnels onto one thing and the peripheral cues vanish, the very cues that would tell you something else is going wrong. Stress can come from the job (a crushing deadline) or from outside it (a crisis at home), and the worker carrying outside stress onto a precision task is a real, manageable hazard — not a discipline issue.

**Workload — both too much and too little.** *Too much* is obvious: overload makes people shed tasks, and the tasks they shed are the ones that feel "optional" — which are very often the *safety* steps, the double-checks, the verifications. But *too little* is a hazard too: under-load breeds boredom and inattention, and a bored brain misses things just like an overloaded one. The target is a manageable, steady workload — and workload is something you *plan and control*, which makes it a safety control, not a personality trait.

**Time pressure.** *Define it:* the perception that there isn't enough time to do the job properly. This is the accelerant on every other factor. Under time pressure, people switch from careful, step-by-step reasoning to fast pattern-matching (usually right, occasionally catastrophically wrong), they drop the "optional" steps, they take the situational shortcut. Time pressure is where good honest workers get pushed into lapses and situational violations. And often the pressure is *manufactured* — an arbitrary deadline, a "just get it out" — which means it's also *removable*.

> **STOP & THINK**
>
> Notice the common thread: every one of these is a **condition of the work**, not a fixed feature of the worker. Fatigue is set by the roster. Workload and time pressure are set by planning and scheduling. Stress is influenced by how the team and the job are run. That's the whole reason this matters: **if these are conditions, you can manage them — and managing them is a safety control.** "He should have been more alert" mistakes a managed condition for a character flaw. Audit the roster, the schedule, and the workload — not the person's stamina.

So when you investigate an error and you find a fatigued technician on a third consecutive night shift, under time pressure, carrying a heavy workload — you have not found "a careless person." You've found a *stack of performance-shaping factors* that any normal human would have erred under. That's not an excuse for the person; it's the *actual location of the problem*, and every one of those factors is something the organization controls.

### 8. Industry example

AeroMach has a recurring spike of small errors on the back half of night shifts during high-demand months. The old read: "night-shift people are sloppier." Dana maps the performance-shaping factors instead. Fatigue: techs on their third or fourth consecutive night, working against the body clock. Time pressure: a month-end push to clear the backlog. Workload: short-staffed, so each person carries more. Of *course* the errors spiked — those are exactly the conditions that load the dice. The fix isn't a stern memo about alertness. It's roster limits on consecutive nights, capping the month-end surge per shift, and pulling in extra hands for the push. The error rate falls — because they treated the conditions as the hazard. Priya's takeaway: "We didn't have a sloppy-people problem. We had a fatigue-and-overload problem wearing a people-problem costume."

- A) A permanent personality trait
- B) A manageable condition (internal or external) that raises or lowers the likelihood of error
- C) A type of machine fault
- D) A disciplinary category

> **Best answer: B** — They're controllable conditions that load the dice, not fixed traits.

**Q2. A fatigued person is dangerous partly because:**
- A) They always know they're impaired
- B) They are the worst judge of their own fatigue — performance degrades while they feel fine — so fatigue must be managed by scheduling, not self-assessment
- C) Fatigue only affects new staff
- D) Fatigue improves judgement

> **Best answer: B** — You can't ask your way out of fatigue risk; you schedule your way out.

**Q3. When workload is too high, the tasks people tend to drop first are:**
- A) The easy ones
- B) The "optional"-feeling steps — which are often the safety checks and verifications
- C) Random
- D) None — people just work faster safely

> **Best answer: B** — Overload sheds the steps that feel skippable, and those are frequently the safety steps.

**Q4. The safety significance of time pressure is that it:**
- A) Has no effect on error
- B) Accelerates every other factor — pushing people into fast pattern-matching, dropped steps, and situational shortcuts — and is often manufactured and removable
- C) Always improves performance
- D) Only matters to managers

> **Best answer: B** — Time pressure is the accelerant; much of it is arbitrary and can be removed.

### 7. The teach-out

One performance-shaping factor deserves its own moment because it's so quiet you don't feel it happening: **complacency.** *Define it:* a state of reduced attention and lowered vigilance that creeps in when a task is familiar, routine, and has "always been fine." It's the opposite of overload — nothing is going wrong, the job is easy, you've done it a thousand times, and your guard quietly drops.

Here's the counter-intuitive, important part: **experience can *increase* complacency.** The expert who has done this install ten thousand times stops actively looking, because nothing has ever bitten them. That's the same autopilot that produces slips — familiarity breeds the routine that lets attention wander. Complacency is dangerous precisely because it doesn't *feel* dangerous; there's no stress, no overload, no alarm. The defence isn't "care more" (a wish again). It's structural: confirmation steps, independent checks, and deliberately treating routine flight-critical tasks as if they're *not* routine — because the consequence of getting one wrong is never routine.

Now let me hand you a practical tool that working maintenance and safety people carry in their heads — the **Dirty Dozen.** *Define it:* a well-known list of twelve common preconditions for human error — the recurring conditions that, again and again, sit behind maintenance and operational errors. You'll recognize most of them already from this course:

- **Lack of communication** · **Complacency** · **Lack of knowledge** · **Distraction** · **Lack of teamwork** · **Fatigue** · **Lack of resources** · **Pressure** · **Lack of assertiveness** · **Stress** · **Lack of awareness** · **Norms** (the unwritten "how we do it here," i.e. routine violations).

> **PAUSE & APPLY**
>
> The Dirty Dozen isn't a new theory — it's a *pocket checklist*. After an error, or before a high-risk task, run down the twelve and ask "which of these are present right now?" You'll almost always find two or three loaded. That scan is a fast, practical way to surface the performance-shaping factors and the SHELL interfaces we've been building — it's the same ideas, organized as a field-usable list. Notice how it cross-links: "Pressure," "Fatigue," "Stress" are performance-shaping factors; "Norms" is routine violations; "Lack of communication/teamwork/assertiveness" is the Liveware–Liveware interface; "Lack of awareness" previews situational awareness.

A scope note, so you know where this fits in the catalogue: this lesson gives you the Dirty Dozen as a *scan tool* inside the human-factors foundation. The dedicated course, **AQ-086 The Dirty Dozen**, takes each of the twelve apart in depth with its own countermeasures. If the twelve become central to your role, that's your next course. Here, they're a useful checklist that ties the whole section together.

### 8. Industry example

AeroMach's most-experienced technician — the one everyone trusts — produces a rare error on a routine, familiar install. The investigation finds no fatigue, no overload, no time pressure. What it finds is **complacency**: a task done so many times that the verification had become a glance, not a check. Dana doesn't "retrain" a thirty-year veteran on a task he could do in his sleep — that would miss the point entirely. She adds an *independent* confirmation step on that flight-critical feature, so the control no longer depends on one expert's vigilance staying perfect forever. Running the Dirty Dozen, the loaded item was "Complacency," full stop — and the fix matched. Priya's note: "The danger wasn't that he was bad at it. The danger was that he was *so good at it* he'd stopped looking."
