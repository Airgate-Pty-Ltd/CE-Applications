# Section 2 — Error Types: Why Competent People Err

> **Section goal**
>
> Break the single word "error" into its real types — slips, lapses, mistakes, and violations — because each has a different cause and a different fix. The learner leaves able to classify an error and explain why "they made a mistake" is usually imprecise and unhelpful.

**LAYER B — TEACH THE SUBJECT**

### 7. The teach-out

"They made an error" is about as useful as a doctor saying "you're sick." Sick *how*? Because the cause and the cure are completely different depending on the *type* of error. Let me give you the three honest-mistake types first; violations get their own lesson because they're a different animal.

Start with a distinction that organizes everything: did the person have the **right plan** or the **wrong plan**?

**Slips and lapses are right-plan, wrong-execution.** The person knew exactly what to do — the intention was correct — but the *doing* went wrong. These are failures of attention and memory, and they hit *experienced* people doing *routine, automatic* tasks. That's the counter-intuitive part: skill doesn't protect you from slips, it slightly *increases* them, because experts run on autopilot.

- **A slip** is an action that goes wrong — your hands do the wrong thing. You reach for the correct tool and grab the one next to it. You torque to a familiar value instead of the one this job actually called for. The plan was right; the action slipped.
- **A lapse** is a memory failure — a step gets *omitted*. You get interrupted mid-sequence and, when you come back, you skip the step you'd already mentally checked off. The classic lapse is the *left-out* step: not done wrong, just not done. Interruptions are the number-one cause.

**Mistakes are wrong-plan.** Here the execution may be flawless — the person did exactly what they intended — but the *intention itself* was wrong. They misjudged the situation, applied the wrong rule, or didn't know the right one. A mistake is a *thinking* failure, not an attention failure. It hits people who are reasoning their way through an unfamiliar or ambiguous situation — often the *less* experienced, or anyone facing something new.

> **STOP & THINK**
>
> Why does this taxonomy matter so much? Because **each type has a different fix, and using the wrong fix wastes effort and changes nothing.**
> - Slips and lapses (right plan, wrong execution) are beaten by **design**: checklists, no-interruption zones, confirmation steps, mistake-proofing the action, place-keeping.
> - Mistakes (wrong plan) are beaten by **knowledge and decision support**: better training, clearer procedures, decision aids, a second opinion, time to think.
> "Retrain the person" is the right answer for a *mistake* and the *wrong* answer for a *lapse* — you can't train someone out of being interrupted. Misclassify the error and you fix the wrong thing.

So when someone says "the technician made a mistake," your trained reflex should be: *was it actually a mistake — a wrong plan — or was it a slip or a lapse, where the plan was right and execution failed?* That single question changes what you do next. It's the difference between sending someone back to training they didn't need and putting a no-interruption zone on the bench where the real problem was.

### 8. Industry example

Three AeroMach events, three types. (1) A technician torques a fastener to a value he's used a thousand times — but *this* assembly spec called for a different value. Right intention, wrong action: a **slip**. (2) On a different job, a tech is pulled away mid-sequence for a tooling question, returns, and signs off the assembly with one verification step never performed. Right plan, omitted step: a **lapse** — and the interruption is the cause, not the person. (3) A newer tech, facing an ambiguous traveller, reasons that two similar processes are interchangeable and applies the wrong one to the letter. Flawless execution of a wrong plan: a **mistake**. Dana's point to Priya: "Three 'errors,' three different fixes. The slip needs the value confirmed at the tool. The lapse needs an interruption protocol. The mistake needs a clearer traveller and training. If we'd just 'retrained' all three, we'd have fixed exactly one of them."

### 14. Reflection
> Take your Lesson 0.1 error again. Was it a slip (wrong action, right plan), a lapse (omitted step, right plan), or a mistake (wrong plan)? Does the fix that was applied actually match the type?

### 15. Knowledge Check — graded • 4 questions • 80% to pass

**Q1. A slip and a lapse are both:**
- A) Failures of the plan itself
- B) Right-plan, wrong-execution failures of attention or memory
- C) Deliberate rule-breaking
- D) Only made by untrained people

> **Best answer: B** — Slips and lapses are correct-intention failures in the *doing*.

**Q2. An experienced technician gets interrupted and omits a verification step he'd normally do. This is a:**
- A) Mistake (wrong plan)
- B) Lapse (right plan, a step omitted — typically caused by interruption)
- C) Violation
- D) Hardware fault

> **Best answer: B** — An omitted step from a memory failure, driven by interruption, is a lapse.

**Q3. A "mistake" in this taxonomy means:**
- A) Any error of any kind
- B) A wrong-plan error — the intention itself was wrong (misjudged the situation or applied the wrong rule), even if executed perfectly
- C) A typo
- D) A slip of the hand

> **Best answer: B** — A mistake is a thinking/planning failure, distinct from a slip or lapse.

**Q4. Why does classifying the error type matter?**
- A) It doesn't — all errors get retraining
- B) Each type has a different fix; slips/lapses need design controls, mistakes need knowledge/decision support — misclassify and you fix the wrong thing
- C) It's only for paperwork
- D) It decides who to discipline

> **Best answer: B** — The taxonomy points you to the *right* control. "Retrain" fixes a mistake, not a lapse.

## Lesson 2.2 — Violations: When the Rule Itself Is the Problem

**LAYER B — TEACH THE SUBJECT**

### 7. The teach-out

Slips, lapses, and mistakes are all *unintended* — nobody meant to do them. A **violation** is different: it's a **deliberate deviation from a rule, procedure, or standard.** The person chose, knowingly, not to follow the process. And the instinct, the moment you hear "deliberate," is to reach for discipline. Hold that instinct, because here's the thing that surprises every new safety officer: **most violations are not malicious, and treating them as malice keeps you from fixing them.**

Let me define the common kinds in plain terms.

**A routine violation** is a shortcut that has quietly become "how we do it here." The official procedure says one thing; the floor has drifted to an easier way that *usually* works, everybody does it, and nobody gets corrected. Routine violations are a *cultural* signal — they tell you the procedure and the reality have drifted apart, often because the procedure is unrealistic, outdated, or harder than it needs to be.

**A situational violation** happens when the conditions make following the rule nearly impossible *right now* — the proper tool is unavailable, the time pressure is crushing, the staffing is short — so the person bends the rule to get the job done. The violation is the *symptom*; the impossible situation is the *disease*.

**A necessary (or exceptional) violation** is when following the rule would actually be *worse* — a genuine emergency or an edge case the procedure never anticipated, where breaking the rule is the safer choice. These are rare, and they often reveal a gap in the procedures rather than a flaw in the person.

> **STOP & THINK**
>
> Here's the reframe that matters: a violation — especially a *routine* one — is usually a message about your **system**, not a verdict on your worker. If everyone is taking the same shortcut, the question is not "why are all these people bad?" It's "why is the official way so unrealistic that compliance became the exception?" The fix for a routine violation is rarely discipline. It's: make the right way the easy way, or fix the procedure so the right way is also the workable way. Discipline one person for a routine violation and the violation continues — you've just taught everyone to hide it.

This is exactly where the distinction *between a violation and an honest mistake* earns its keep — and it's also where **just culture** lives, which we'll build out fully in Section 5. The short version now: an honest slip, lapse, or mistake should never be punished — punishing normal error just drives it underground. A violation needs to be *understood* — why did the person feel they had to deviate? Only a small minority of cases — reckless disregard, true sabotage — warrant a disciplinary response, and you can't tell which is which unless people feel safe enough to tell you the truth. Blame is the enemy of information, and information is the only thing that makes you safer.

### 8. Industry example

At AeroMach, Sam the supervisor notices the whole second shift has stopped using the proper torque-sequence checklist on a particular assembly — they've memorized a faster order. A *routine violation*. The blame instinct says "write them up." Dana digs instead: the checklist was written for an older fixture and now lists the steps in an awkward order that genuinely slows the job and the team "fixed" it themselves. The real finding isn't a bad shift — it's an outdated procedure that made compliance the slow, painful option. They rewrite the checklist to match the current fixture and the *correct* sequence becomes the *fast* sequence. The violation disappears — because they removed the reason for it. Dana's line to Priya: "When everybody breaks the same rule, audit the rule."

- A) Always malicious
- B) A *deliberate* deviation from a rule or procedure (the others are unintended)
- C) Impossible to prevent
- D) Only committed by managers

> **Best answer: B** — Violations are intentional deviations; slips/lapses/mistakes are unintended.

**Q2. A "routine violation" is best understood as:**
- A) Sabotage
- B) A shortcut that has become normal practice — usually a signal the procedure and reality have drifted apart
- C) A one-time emergency action
- D) An honest memory lapse

> **Best answer: B** — Routine violations are a cultural/system signal, not just individual misbehaviour.

**Q3. Most violations are:**
- A) Malicious and deserve immediate discipline
- B) Not malicious — they're driven by unrealistic procedures, impossible conditions, or normalized shortcuts, so the system usually needs the fix
- C) Random
- D) Impossible to understand

> **Best answer: B** — Treating non-malicious violations as malice hides the system problem you actually need to fix.

**Q4. When an entire shift takes the same shortcut, the best first move is to:**
- A) Discipline the shift
